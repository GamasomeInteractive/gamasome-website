A humanoid team we spoke with had a bimanual policy that looked finished. Standing at a bench, the robot could take a tote from one hand, steady it with the other, and set it down cleanly most of the time. Then the task changed slightly: pick the tote up from a low shelf, turn, and carry it through a doorway to a counter.

The arms did exactly what they had been trained to do. The problem was everything else. Reaching low pushed the center of mass forward, the turn happened while both hands were loaded, and the doorway forced a narrower stance than anything in the training set. The policy had never seen its own legs matter, because every demonstration had been recorded with the robot planted in one spot.

That is the core issue with data collection for humanoid robots. Most of what the robotics industry knows about collecting training data was learned on arms bolted to tables. A humanoid inherits all of those problems and adds several of its own that no arm ever has.

## Why humanoid data is a different problem, not a bigger one

The obvious difference is size. A typical industrial arm has a handful of joints. The Unitree G1 used in the [Humanoid Everyday dataset](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.08807) is a 29-DoF robot, paired with 7-DoF three-fingered hands. Figure says its Helix model [coordinates a 35-DoF action space at 200Hz](https://www.figure.ai/news/helix), and that is for the upper body only: desired wrist poses, finger flexion and abduction, and torso and head orientation targets.

But dimension count is the less interesting difference. The more important one is coupling. An arm's base never moves, so its joints can be treated as a chain hanging off something fixed. A humanoid has no fixed base. Every arm motion changes where the body's weight sits, and the legs have to compensate. One published [data collection guide for the Booster K1 humanoid](https://roboticscenter.ai/hardware/booster-k1/data-collection) makes exactly this point: arm movements shift the center of mass, so the locomotion controller has to compensate continuously during teleoperation.

That coupling is why you cannot just collect arm data and bolt a walking controller underneath. The datasets themselves show the gap. The Humanoid Everyday authors put it bluntly in their abstract:

> The majority of current robot learning datasets and benchmarks mainly focus on stationary robot arms.
>
> *Humanoid Everyday research team, [arXiv, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08807)*

They go further: existing humanoid datasets are often confined to fixed environments or limited in task diversity, and they frequently lack human-humanoid interaction and lower-body locomotion. In other words, the parts of the problem that make a humanoid a humanoid are the parts least represented in the data.

## The five data needs of a humanoid, ranked by how much simulation can carry

Rather than treat humanoid data as one undifferentiated pile, it helps to split it by *what the policy is learning* and then ask a single practical question of each piece: can simulation produce most of this, or does it have to come from the real world? The answer changes from one need to the next, and it should drive where your collection budget goes.

The ranking is the useful part. Need 01 is largely a compute problem. Need 05 is entirely a real-world collection problem. Everything between is a blend, and the blend shifts toward real capture as you move down the list.

## How humanoid data needs compare with other robot forms

Putting the humanoid next to the robots most data programs were built for makes the extra burden concrete.

| Data need | Fixed arm | Mobile manipulator | Quadruped | Humanoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Balance and fall recovery** | None, base is fixed | Minimal, wheeled base is statically stable | Yes, but four contact points give wide margins | Yes, on two feet with a narrow support base |
| **Bimanual coordination** | Only in dual-arm cells | Sometimes, if fitted with two arms | Rarely, most have no arms | Core requirement, often with dexterous hands |
| **Whole-body coupling** | None | Weak, base and arm usually controlled separately | Locomotion only | Strong, arm motion shifts the center of mass |
| **Human-scale spaces** | Workcell only | Flat floors, ramps, wide aisles | Rough terrain, some stairs | Stairs, doorways, counters, shelves at every height |
| **Action space** | A handful of joints | Arm plus base velocity | Legs only | Dozens of joints, e.g. 29 DoF body on a Unitree G1 |
| **Where data mostly comes from** | Teleoperation, kinesthetic teaching | Teleoperation plus navigation logs | Simulation and RL | All of the above, plus human video |

The bottom row is the real takeaway. Every other form can get away with one or two dominant data sources. A humanoid needs nearly all of them, because it is effectively several robot problems sharing one body.

## Where humanoid data actually comes from

The methods are the same ones used across robotics. What changes is how they are weighted and what each one has to capture.

### Teleoperation on the target humanoid

Still the anchor. Figure has said Helix was trained on [roughly 500 hours of teleoperated behavior data](https://www.figure.ai/news/helix), collected across multiple robots and multiple operators. For a humanoid, the hard part is the interface: VR headsets and hand trackers capture the upper body well, but faithfully capturing lower-body intent while the operator is also controlling two hands is a genuinely open design problem, which is exactly what the OpenHLM work above is probing. If you are scoping this, our [teleoperation data collection](/services/teleoperation-data-collection/) practice and the broader guide to [human demonstrations for robot training](/blog/human-demonstrations-for-robot-training/) cover the operational side.

### Human video, which matters more for humanoids than for any other form

Here the humanoid has a structural advantage no other robot has: its body roughly matches the bodies in the video. Figure's [Project Go-Big](https://www.figure.ai/news/project-go-big) is the clearest example. The company reports that after training exclusively on egocentric human video, captured passively in real homes through its Brookfield partnership, Helix could navigate cluttered spaces from commands like "go to the fridge," and that this required no robot demonstrations whatsoever. That is a company claim without independent replication so far, but it directly targets Need 04.

Tesla has reportedly moved in the same direction. Business Insider reported that the Optimus team [shifted away from motion-capture suits and teleoperation](https://www.aol.com/tesla-shifted-optimus-training-strategy-092502010.html) toward video of workers performing tasks, citing the ability to scale collection faster, while noting the trade-off that video is less robust than the more expensive methods.

### Simulation and synthetic trajectories

Simulation carries most of Need 01 and a growing share of the rest. NVIDIA's [GR00T N1 paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.14734) describes a data pyramid with web and human video at the base, synthetic data in the middle, and real robot data at the top, and reports using generated neural trajectories to expand its teleoperation data from 88 hours to 827 hours. Those are NVIDIA's own figures, and how well that kind of multiplication holds up across tasks is still an open research question. The broader trade-offs are covered in [synthetic vs real-world data for robotics](/blog/synthetic-vs-real-world-data-robotics/).

  <p className="text-gray-700">Human video is cheap, but it carries no robot action labels. Someone, or some model, has to infer what the robot's joints should have done. GR00T N1 handles this with a latent-action codebook and an inverse dynamics model trained on real robot data to infer pseudo-actions. It does not replace it.</p>

## What teams get wrong about humanoid data

  <div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6">
    <h4 className="mb-2 text-[16px] font-bold text-gray-900">Recording the robot standing still</h4>
    <p className="text-[14.5px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600">Bench-height bimanual demos with the robot planted are the easiest data to collect and the least representative of deployment. If the real task involves walking, turning, or reaching low, a meaningful share of demonstrations has to include those motions, or the legs never become part of the learned skill.</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6">
    <h4 className="mb-2 text-[16px] font-bold text-gray-900">Logging hands but not feet</h4>
    <p className="text-[14.5px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600">Many capture specs inherited from arm programs log end-effector poses and cameras, then drop lower-body joint states and foot contact to save bandwidth. For a humanoid that is the part of the episode that explains why a grasp failed. Log the whole body, on one clock, every time.</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6">
    <h4 className="mb-2 text-[16px] font-bold text-gray-900">Collecting in one beautiful lab</h4>
    <p className="text-[14.5px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600">A humanoid's value is that it works in spaces built for people, and people's spaces vary wildly. Door widths, stair rises, counter heights, lighting, clutter. A dataset recorded in one well-lit lab teaches the policy one building. Environment diversity is a first-class collection target, not a nice-to-have.</p>
  </div>
  <div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6">
    <h4 className="mb-2 text-[16px] font-bold text-gray-900">Treating falls and recoveries as waste</h4>
    <p className="text-[14.5px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600">A stumble that the robot recovers from is some of the most valuable data a humanoid program produces, and the easiest to discard as a failed take. Keep near-falls, recoveries, and corrected grasps, labeled as such. A success-only dataset teaches nothing about getting out of trouble.</p>
  </div>
</div>

These are the same quality disciplines that apply to every physical AI program, covered in more depth in [physical AI data quality](/blog/physical-ai-data-quality/), with one addition: on a humanoid, the definition of "the whole episode" is the whole body.

  <p className="mb-4 text-xs font-semibold tracking-[0.08em] text-[#000B71] uppercase">Why this matters for budgets</p>
  <p className="text-gray-700">The five needs are not equally expensive. Balance data is mostly GPU time. Human-scale environment and human-interaction data are mostly site access, operator hours, and logistics, the kind of cost that does not fall when compute gets cheaper. A collection plan that splits budget evenly across modalities will usually overspend on what simulation already handles and starve the two needs that only real-world capture can fill.</p>

## Planning data collection for humanoid robots in practice

Start by naming the deployment task precisely and walking it through the five needs. A humanoid that sorts parts at a fixed station is mostly a Need 02 problem with light locomotion. A humanoid that carries totes between rooms in an occupied building touches all five, and its data plan should look completely different.

Then decide, for each need, where the data comes from. Lean on simulation and RL for balance, and validate it on hardware. Use teleoperation on the target robot for bimanual and whole-body skills, with an interface that exposes the legs as well as the hands. Use human video and multi-site real capture for environment diversity. And budget explicitly for collecting around real people, because no other source will give you that data.

Real deployments show why the narrow version comes first. The best-documented humanoid production work so far, BMW's Figure pilot at Spartanburg covered in our guide to [physical AI in manufacturing](/blog/physical-ai-in-manufacturing/), was one bounded task, picking sheet metal and placing it for welding, held reliably over months. That is a deliberate choice of a task where the hardest data needs stay manageable. General-purpose humanoids will need the bottom of the list too, and that part of the dataset does not yet exist at scale anywhere.

The teams that get there first will not be the ones with the most hours. They will be the ones whose hours cover the whole body, in real human spaces, with people in the frame.
