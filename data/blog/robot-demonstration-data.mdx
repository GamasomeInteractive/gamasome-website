Open the actual file behind a robot demonstration for the first time and it rarely looks like what the word suggests. There's no "picking up a mug" anywhere in it. What's there is a state array of thirty-two numbers per timestep, an action array of a similar size, a handful of camera streams stored as compressed byte strings, and a stack of timestamps that don't quite line up because three different sensors were sampling at three different rates.

That gap, between what a demonstration looks like to a person and what it actually is to a training pipeline, is the subject of this guide. Most discussions of robot data focus on how it gets collected, teleoperation, kinesthetic teaching, handheld capture, which we cover in depth in [human demonstrations for robot training](/blog/human-demonstrations-for-robot-training/). This one is about the artifact itself. What's inside a demonstration once it's been recorded, how it's structured, and why that structure is what determines whether a policy can actually learn anything from it.

It is not raw video. A trainable episode pairs synchronized observations with an action vector, a temporal structure describing what phase of the task is happening, and an outcome label, all tied together by consistent timestamps and episode metadata. ## A video is evidence. A demonstration is a dataset.

A video of someone picking up a mug is useful evidence that the task is physically possible. It is not training data, because nothing in a video file tells a policy what joint torques, what gripper width, or what wrist angle produced that motion. A robot has to predict an action, frame by frame, and a video alone gives it nothing to predict against.

A robot demonstration closes that gap by recording the action alongside the observation, every time. That's true whether the demonstration came from teleoperation, where the action is literally what the operator commanded the robot to do, or from another capture method that retargets a human motion into the robot's own action space. The point of the artifact is the pairing, not the footage.

## Anatomy of a demonstration: five fields every trainable episode carries

Regardless of which dataset format a team uses, a trainable episode is built around the same five kinds of information. Thinking of it this way makes it obvious what's missing when a "dataset" turns out not to train anything.

*Every layer depends on the one below it sharing the same clock. A demonstration missing any one of these five fields is footage, not training data.*

## Why the shared clock is the hardest part, not the hardest-looking part

None of the five fields above are individually difficult to record. A camera captures frames, a robot reports its own joint encoder values, an operator or a model produces an action, a reviewer assigns a label. What's genuinely difficult is that these streams rarely sample at the same rate. A camera might run at thirty frames a second, joint state might be logged at a much higher control-loop frequency, and a force-torque sensor, if one is present, often runs at yet another rate entirely.

That mismatch is why serious demonstration formats keep a separate timestamp array for each modality rather than assuming everything lines up by index position. An episode where frame 400 of the camera stream doesn't actually correspond to the same instant as entry 400 of the action array isn't subtly wrong. It's unusable, because the policy would be trained to associate an action with the wrong observation, and nothing about the file structure would reveal that until a trained model behaves strangely for reasons nobody can trace. Getting the shared clock right at capture time is unglamorous work. It is also the single most common point of silent failure in demonstration data, more common than a bad label or a missing frame, because a timing bug produces a file that opens cleanly and looks complete.

## Two common containers for the same five fields

| Layout | How trajectories are stored | How it handles multi-rate sensors | Use it when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Per-episode HDF5 | One file per demonstration, with state, action, and timestamp data in separate internal groups | Each modality keeps its own timestamp array, since cameras, joints, and force sensors often sample at different rates | You want a self-contained file per episode and fine control over custom fields |
| LeRobot dataset format | Per-episode or chunked Parquet tables for numeric data, with video stored separately in its own chunked folders | A shared frame index ties observation, state, and action together; metadata files separately track task and episode-level information | You want a format with broad tooling support across the open robot learning ecosystem |

It's worth noting that even within "the LeRobot format," the exact file layout has shifted between versions. Earlier releases stored one Parquet file per episode alongside separate JSONL files for episode and task metadata. More recent versions consolidate many episodes into larger chunked Parquet files for efficiency, with per-episode records like length and task assignment stored as their own chunked table rather than a single flat file. The underlying five fields don't change. Which files they live in does, which is exactly why checking a dataset's own metadata for its format version matters more than assuming a folder structure from memory.

## Where this fits into the rest of the pipeline

A well-formed demonstration is the raw material, not the finished product. Whether that material gets collected through teleoperation, where the action vector is quite literally what a human commanded in real time, or another method entirely, is the subject of [teleoperation data collection](/blog/what-is-teleoperation-data-collection/) and, more broadly, [human demonstrations for robot training](/blog/human-demonstrations-for-robot-training/). Whether the resulting episodes are actually good enough to train on, beyond just being structurally complete, is the question answered in [robot training data collection](/blog/robot-training-data-collection/).

The practical takeaway for a team looking at its own dataset today is simple: open a handful of episodes and check, field by field, that all five are actually there, synchronized, and labeled consistently. A dataset that passes that check is raw material a policy can learn from. One that doesn't is footage with extra steps, and no amount of additional volume fixes that on its own.
