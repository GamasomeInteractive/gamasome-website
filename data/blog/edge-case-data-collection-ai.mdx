At CES 2026, Motional's CEO told a room full of reporters something most autonomous vehicle companies don't say out loud. [Less than 1 percent of the driving data the company's fleet collects is actually useful](https://www.techbrew.com/stories/2026/02/05/autonomous-vehicles-edge-cases). The other 99-plus percent is highway cruising, clear intersections, and routine merges, the kind of driving any reasonably trained system already handles without thinking.

That admission is why Motional built a system called Omnitag, whose entire job is mining the useful clips out of the haystack automatically. It is also the quiet, expensive truth sitting underneath nearly every serious autonomy program, in cars and in warehouses alike. Volume was never the hard part. Finding and capturing the rare moment that actually teaches the model something new is.

This article is about **edge case data collection**: the deliberate work of finding, triggering, staging, and sometimes simulating the scenarios that sit in the long tail of a model's experience. It is a close cousin of the general real-world robustness problem we cover in [how physical AI handles unpredictable environments](/blog/physical-ai-real-world-environments/), but it is a narrower, sharper question. Not "how do we make robots robust to surprise in general," but "how do we actually get our hands on the rare, dangerous, boundary-case data before it bites us in deployment."

  <p className="text-gray-700"><strong className="font-semibold text-gray-900">Edge case data collection</strong> is the practice of deliberately finding and capturing the rare, high-impact scenarios a robot or autonomous system meets in the real world but almost never sees in routine training data. It is not about recording more hours. It is about mining what you already recorded, building triggers that flag the moment something unusual happens, staging confirmed weak points on purpose, and simulating what is too rare or dangerous to stage for real, then holding a slice of it back so you can actually prove a fix worked.</p>

## The real problem isn't volume, it's visibility

Ask most teams how confident they are in their data and they'll point to a counter: hours logged, miles driven, episodes recorded. That number feels reassuring. It is also close to meaningless on its own, because the scenarios that actually decide whether a system is safe are rare by definition, and rare things stay rare no matter how long you keep the camera rolling.

[Voxel51 frames this clearly](https://voxel51.com/blog/long-tail-autonomous-driving): the bottleneck in autonomous driving has shifted from gathering data to finding the valuable cases already buried inside it, because collecting more just makes the search harder. A rare object, like a donkey wandering onto a road, is unusual. A rare scenario is often just ordinary objects in an unusual arrangement, like a cyclist carrying a ladder. Nothing about that scene looks exotic in a single frame. It is the combination that breaks a model, and combinations don't show up in a keyword search.

That search problem has a real cost attached to it. An iMerit product leader quoted in the same CES 2026 coverage put the automotive reliability bar at [roughly 99.997 percent](https://www.techbrew.com/stories/2026/02/05/autonomous-vehicles-edge-cases), a number that leaves almost no room for the scenarios a dataset never happened to contain. Dashcam network Nexar says it has collected tens of millions of edge case videos through crowdsourced fleets and uses on-device AI to pick out the valuable clips before they ever leave the device, because shipping everything back to a server would bury the useful footage just as badly as a messy hard drive would. Even the public datasets built specifically to showcase edge cases stay small by comparison. NATIX's open release on Hugging Face runs to [20 minutes of video covering 21 events](https://huggingface.co/datasets/natix-network-org/natix-edge-case-driving-dataset), which is itself a demonstration of how thin this slice of the world really is.

## The edge case capture ladder: five rungs from passive logging to deliberate provocation

Every method for finding rare scenarios trades effort for certainty. At the bottom, you log everything and hope the valuable moment is in there somewhere. At the top, you go out and deliberately provoke the scenario you already suspect is a problem. Thinking of the methods as a ladder, not a menu, makes it obvious why mature programs climb through several rungs on the same scenario instead of picking one and stopping.

    <p className="text-[14.5px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600">A dashboard that reports millions of hours collected looks impressive and tells you almost nothing about whether those hours included the lighting condition, the object arrangement, or the human behavior that actually breaks the system. ## Turning captured scenarios into something you can evaluate on

Finding a rare scenario and capturing footage of it is only half the job. The other half, the one almost every program underfunds, is structuring that footage so it can actually prove something later. A clip with no scenario tag, no severity score, and no held-out copy is just an interesting video.

| If you have... | What's actually missing | Start with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A pile of flagged clips from mining or triggers | A consistent way to know if two clips are the same scenario | A scenario taxonomy applied at tagging time, not after the fact |
| One confirmed example of a dangerous edge case | Enough variation for a model to learn a pattern, not one instance | Deliberate staging across lighting, pose, and object variation |
| A scenario too rare or risky to stage for real | Safe coverage without waiting for it to happen again | Simulation, validated against a small real-world sample |
| Training data that already includes some tail cases | Any way to prove a fix worked rather than just assume it | A held-out split from the main training set, reserved for evaluation only |
| A recurring "weird failure" with no clear cause yet | A trigger to catch it live instead of searching for it after the fact | Instrumented logging for disengagements, overrides, or confidence drops |

The categories a vendor blog from Macgence lists as common robotics edge cases, occluded objects in crowded scenes, sudden glare or shadow, damaged or irregularly shaped items, and workers stepping into a robot's path, are a reasonable starting taxonomy. The company also claims addressing those specific outliers improved deployment performance by 35 percent in one case, which is a single, vendor-reported figure that nobody has independently verified, so treat it as a plausibility signal rather than a benchmark.

## What this means for a team starting today

Start by naming the categories of rare scenario your system actually faces, not a generic list copied from somewhere else, and tag every candidate clip to one of them the moment it's flagged. Build at least one trigger into your running system, a disengagement log, a confidence threshold, a human override, because waiting to find rare events through manual review of routine footage is the slowest and most expensive way to find them.

Decide which confirmed scenarios get staged for real and which get simulated first, based on how dangerous or rare they are rather than which is more convenient to set up this week. And before any of that data touches a training run, carve out a held-out slice you will never train on, because that is the only thing standing between "we fixed it" and "we hope we fixed it."

This problem shows up everywhere physical AI meets the real world, not only in cars. The automotive long tail we cover in [data collection in the automotive industry](/blog/data-collection-automotive-industry/) is one of the clearest large-scale examples, where a system running at very high accuracy can still face a meaningful rate of unhandled scenarios per mile driven. And the five sources of surprise we map out in [how physical AI handles unpredictable real-world environments](/blog/physical-ai-real-world-environments/) are a useful lens for deciding which surprises deserve a dedicated edge case program and which are better solved by changing the environment instead.
