A team we have seen building a tabletop manipulation policy did everything the synthetic data pitch promises. They built a simulated version of their work cell, randomized lighting and textures, generated tens of thousands of grasps overnight, and watched the policy hit near-perfect success in simulation. Then they ran it on the real arm. It hesitated over objects it had grasped thousands of times in sim and missed a third of its picks.

The fix was not more simulation. It was a few dozen real demonstrations, recorded on their actual arm, mixed back into the training set. Success on the real robot jumped, and the simulated data, the same data that had failed on its own, suddenly started paying off.

That pattern is the most useful thing to understand about this debate. The question is not which data type wins. It is which one is doing which job, and how far you can lean on the cheap one before the expensive one has to take over.

## What the evidence actually says about co-training

The clearest controlled result comes from the [Sim-and-Real Co-Training study presented at RSS 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.24361v2). Across six tasks on a Franka Panda arm and a GR-1 humanoid, policies trained on real data alone averaged 45.3% success. Adding simulation data pushed that to 83.2%, an average improvement of about 38 points, even though the simulated scenes did not match the real ones exactly.

Two details in that paper matter more than the headline. First, the real data was small: 50 human demonstrations per task on the Panda, 20 on the humanoid. Second, the biggest gains came from simulation built to resemble the real task (the authors call these "digital cousins"), though generic prior simulation data still helped meaningfully.

Industry numbers point the same way. NVIDIA [reported that its GR00T blueprint generated over 750,000 synthetic trajectories in 11 hours](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/accelerate-generalist-humanoid-robot-development-with-nvidia-isaac-gr00t-n1), which it describes as equivalent to 6,500 hours or nine continuous months of human demonstration data. Combining that synthetic data with real data, NVIDIA says, gave GR00T N1 a 40% performance boost over real data alone. That is a vendor-reported result on its own comparison setup, so read it as a strong signal rather than an independent benchmark.

The other side of the ledger is what happens without real data. A 2026 real-to-sim-to-real paper, [DEXTERA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.21045), reported that dexterous policies trained only in simulation reached mean physical success rates between 15.8% and 36.7% zero-shot, depending on the policy architecture. Co-training with a small number of physical demonstrations raised those to between 55.8% and 67.5%.

So the research answer to "which is better" is fairly settled: both, together. What is not settled, and what actually decides whether your program works, is the mix.

## The synthetic share ladder: how much synthetic data is safe to lean on

The mistake most teams make is choosing a ratio once, at the start, and keeping it. The safe share of synthetic data is not a constant. It depends on two things: how much trustworthy real data you already have, and how much of your task's difficulty lives in physics a simulator cannot model well. We frame this as a ladder. Each rung down is a stage or constraint where real data has to carry more of the load.

The ladder is not strictly sequential. A program can sit on rung two for its grasping skill and rung three for its cable routing at the same time. The point is to set the synthetic share per skill and per stage, deliberately, instead of inheriting one global ratio from whatever your first experiment used.

## Why synthetic data vs real-world data is really a question about jobs

Framing the two as competitors hides what each is good at. Synthetic data is unmatched at three jobs: volume, controlled variation, and safe coverage of scenarios nobody wants to stage. Real data is unmatched at one job that matters more than all three: telling you how your robot actually behaves when it touches the world.

Domain randomization is the classic attempt to make synthetic data do both jobs. OpenAI's Dactyl hand, which learned to manipulate a Rubik's Cube, was trained with an automatic version that [started with a single, nonrandomized environment and increased randomization as the network crossed performance thresholds](https://laughingsquid.com/robotic-hand-that-can-solve-rubiks-cubes/), varying properties like object mass, surface friction, joint damping, and actuator forces. Reporting at the time put the simulated experience at [roughly 13,000 years](https://syncedreview.com/2019/10/15/openai-robot-hand-today-rubiks-cube-tomorrow-the-real-world/), and other outlets cite around 10,000. Either way, it is a reminder of how much compute it can take to make simulation cover reality by brute force, for a single task.

Modern co-training is the cheaper path. Instead of randomizing until reality falls inside the distribution, you add a small amount of reality directly. If you want the broader picture of where this fits in a full training recipe, our guide to [physical AI training](/blog/physical-ai-training/) walks through the stages from pretraining to deployment.

    <p className="text-[14.5px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600">When real data is a small fraction of samples, every real episode has outsized influence. That last point is the one we see most often in the field. Teams pour effort into the generation pipeline and then collect their real demonstrations in an afternoon, with inconsistent operators, drifting calibration, and no timestamp discipline. The real data is the anchor. If the anchor is loose, the whole mix drifts.

## A decision table for weighting the mix

| If your situation is... | Weight toward | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No real data yet, still validating the approach | Synthetic, almost entirely | Debug the pipeline cheaply; do not trust the success number |
| Rigid pick-and-place, a few dozen real demos per task | Synthetic-heavy co-training | Published optimums sit at very high sim shares for this task class |
| Cloth, cables, liquids, or tactile-dependent tasks | Real, with synthetic for visual variety | Contact and deformation physics are where simulators fall short |
| A pretrained policy moving into a specific site | Real data from that site | The last mile is your exact hardware, objects, and lighting |
| Rare, dangerous, or expensive-to-stage edge cases | Synthetic, seeded by real failures | Sim is the only safe way to multiply scenarios you cannot stage |
| A large synthetic set but weak real results | More and better real data, not more sim | Adding sim past the optimum can lower real-world success |

## What this means for a team starting today

Start by deciding what your real data has to anchor, not how much synthetic data you can generate. For each skill, place it on the ladder: is it rigid and well modeled, or does its difficulty live in contact and deformation? That answer sets your ceiling on synthetic share far more reliably than any published ratio.

Then budget the real anchor first. Plan for a modest but high-quality set of demonstrations on your target robot, recorded with consistent operators, logged calibration, and hardware-synchronized timestamps. Our guide to [data collection for robotics](/blog/data-collection-for-robotics/) covers what that kind of program costs and where in-house efforts tend to break. If your target is a humanoid, where whole-body teleoperation makes every real episode more expensive, the trade-offs in [data collection for humanoid robots](/blog/data-collection-humanoid-robots/) make the case for careful real-data budgeting even stronger.

Finally, make the mix a measured variable. Hold out real-robot evaluation tasks from day one, sweep the synthetic share on them, and revisit it every time you move a rung down the ladder. Synthetic data is the best tool available for scale. Real-world data is the only thing that tells you whether that scale is pointed in the right direction.
