A manipulation team we advised wired up four capture streams for a single insertion task: a wrist camera, a depth sensor mounted on the gripper, joint encoders on the arm, and a microphone for the operator narrating what they were doing and why. On review day, every clip looked clean. The peg slid into the hole smoothly, over and over, exactly like it should.

The policy trained on that data jammed at the exact moment of contact. Every single time.

Nothing about the footage was visibly wrong. The problem was the clock. Each stream had been timestamped by the software that received it, not by the physical moment it happened, and the small, inconsistent delays between a camera buffer, a sensor driver, and a joint readout had quietly rewritten the story of which action caused which outcome. The data looked synchronized. It was not.

That gap, between footage that looks aligned and data that actually is, is the real subject of multimodal data collection for physical AI. Not how many sensor types you can bolt onto a rig. Whether what they captured can still be trusted once you try to train on all of it at once.

## Multimodal is not a label for "lots of sensors"

Search for "multimodal data collection" and most of what comes back is generic. Glossary entries define multimodal data as information made of several modalities, and vendor pages list text, image, audio, and sensor data as the ingredients, then stop there. That framing is not wrong. It is just not the part of the job that actually determines whether a physical AI model can learn anything useful.

In physical AI specifically, "multimodal" almost always means some combination of three things covered elsewhere in depth: individual sensor streams like depth, force, and LiDAR, which we walk through in [sensor data collection for robotics](/blog/sensor-data-collection-robotics/); video and motion capture, covered fully in [how physical AI processes video and motion data](/blog/physical-ai-video-and-motion-data/); and language, the instructions and annotations that tell a model what a task was meant to accomplish. This piece is not about any one of those in depth. It is about the problem that only shows up once you try to combine them: making sure a frame, a joint angle, a depth reading, and an instruction that were captured within the same few hundred milliseconds of each other are actually treated as describing the same moment.

That alignment work is the operational core of what we mean when we talk about a [multimodal data collection](/services/multimodal-data-collection/) program, as distinct from just owning a lot of sensors.

## The alignment stack: five places multimodal capture actually breaks

Getting several streams into one trainable dataset is not one problem. It is five, and they happen in a fixed order during the life of a capture program. Miss one near the start, and every later stage inherits the error silently, because nothing downstream has a way to tell that the input it received was already wrong.

If any of this sounds like the same discipline that shows up around any single sensor, it is. The difference with multimodal capture is that you are now managing four or five of these problems at once, and they compound. A camera that drifts 10 milliseconds and a depth sensor that drifts 15 milliseconds in the opposite direction do not average out to something fine. They stack into exactly the kind of structured, state-dependent error that is hardest to catch by eye.

## Matching the symptom to the broken layer

When a multimodal policy misbehaves, the fix is rarely "collect more data." It is almost always "find which of the five stages above actually failed."

| Symptom you see in training or evaluation | Layer that is actually broken | Where to look first |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Policy looks perfect in review clips, fails at the exact moment of contact | Temporal synchronization | Hardware trigger vs. software-stamped timestamps |
| Depth or point-cloud overlay looks shifted, and worse after weeks of use | Spatial calibration | Re-run extrinsics, log a fresh baseline per device |
| Model seems to only react to the fastest-updating sensor | Format and rate normalization | Per-modality buffering instead of one forced shared tick |
| Model ignores spoken or written instructions when the scene alone predicts the action | Semantic and language alignment | Instruction diversity and grounding, not just presence |
| Some sessions train well and others quietly hurt performance, with no clear pattern | Quality gating | Alignment checks run at capture time, not after the fact |

## What this means for a team starting today

If you are deep in getting one modality right, that is the right place to be. Our guides to [sensor data collection for robotics](/blog/sensor-data-collection-robotics/) and [how physical AI processes video and motion data](/blog/physical-ai-video-and-motion-data/) go further into each of those individually. This piece exists for the moment after that, when those streams have to agree with each other inside a single dataset.

Three things are worth deciding before a multimodal capture session is scheduled, not after. Pick a reference clock and a synchronization method, hardware trigger or software timestamp, and know its measured jitter on your actual rig rather than its spec sheet number. Decide on one action and language schema before the first operator starts, because three teams describing "grasp" three different ways do not produce one dataset, they produce three incompatible ones sharing a folder. And build the alignment check into the capture session itself, the way ForEnt's sit-to-stand motion did, instead of discovering the gap once a policy is already failing in a hard-to-explain way.

The teams getting this right are not the ones with the most sensor types bolted onto a rig. They are the ones who treat synchronization, calibration, format, and language as one connected discipline rather than four separate afterthoughts. That is exactly the gap our [multimodal data collection](/services/multimodal-data-collection/) team is built to close, whether that means designing the capture rig, running the alignment checks, or auditing a dataset you already have before you train on it again.
