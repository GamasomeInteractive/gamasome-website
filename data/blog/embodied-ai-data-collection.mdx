Open X-Embodiment is free, it covers more than twenty robot embodiments, and a team can download it this afternoon. A manipulation startup we spoke with did exactly that: trained a pick-and-place policy on a slice of it, watched it perform respectably on held-out benchmark tasks, then shipped it to their own warehouse pilot. The robot could not reliably pick up their own bins.

Nothing about the dataset was wrong. The policy had simply never seen their gripper geometry, their lighting, or their specific object mix, because no public dataset was built around their robot. That is the quiet, expensive lesson sitting underneath most embodied AI data programs: pooled data gets you started, and it almost never finishes the job.

This guide is about what actually has to happen between "we have a robot and a task" and "we have data a policy can learn from." Not the marketing version. The operational one.

It cannot be scraped the way internet text or images can. ## Why this data cannot just be downloaded

Language models had the advantage of an internet that already existed. Someone else had already written billions of sentences before anyone thought to train a model on them. Embodied AI has no equivalent. A <a href="https://sjcj.nuaa.edu.cn/sjcjyclen/article/abstract/202602004" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="text-[#000B71] underline underline-offset-2 hover:text-sky-600">2026 survey on embodied intelligence datasets</a> puts the structural problem plainly: unlike natural language processing and computer vision, which can lean on massive internet-scale corpora directly, data collection for embodied intelligence generally requires physical interaction between an agent and its environment.

That single fact explains nearly everything else in this article. It explains why collection is expensive. It explains why pooled datasets lean so heavily toward kitchen counters and tabletop pick-and-place, because those are the easiest scenes to stage repeatedly. And it explains why, by most industry accounts, demand for usable physical interaction data is running far ahead of supply. Estimates that circulated through Chinese trade press in early 2026 put the global stock of high-quality, real-world physical interaction data at around 500,000 hours, against an industry-cited demand closer to 10 million hours. Several outlets repeat a near-identical figure, which suggests a shared origin rather than independent audits, so treat the exact numbers as a directional signal. The direction itself, a gap measured in millions of hours, is the part worth taking seriously.

## The collection stack: five layers, and nobody lives on just one

Every embodied AI data source trades fidelity for scale somewhere. Framing the four or five common methods as a stack, from broadest and cheapest at the bottom to most precise and most expensive at the top, makes that trade-off explicit and makes it obvious why real programs blend layers instead of picking a favorite.

Research framing backs up the shape of this stack, not just the practitioner intuition behind it. A separate 2026 survey on the [data pyramid for embodied manipulation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24744) treats real-robot interaction data as the most direct supervision available for learning manipulation policies, with human video and embodiment-agnostic demonstrations forming broader, lower-fidelity layers underneath it. Different vocabulary, same underlying trade-off.

## What industrial-scale collection actually looks like right now

If the stack above feels abstract, the current Chinese embodied AI sector is running a live, very large experiment in filling it. It is worth understanding, because it previews where the rest of the industry is probably headed on cost and method.

[JD.com has committed to mobilizing more than 100,000 of its own employees](https://www.itiger.com/news/1100161095) and, by some reporting, up to 500,000 outside participants across its retail, logistics, food delivery, and home services businesses, to collect physical interaction data. The stated goal is five million hours of human-scene video within a year and more than ten million within two, alongside a separate target of one million hours of robot body data. In Suqian, JD and the local government set up a data collection community where participants wear the JoyEgoCam, which records upper-limb trajectories, force distribution, and human-environment interaction, while doing ordinary household chores. The "up to 500,000" figure is itself a ceiling in some reporting rather than a confirmed enrollment number, worth noting if you see it quoted as a hard fact elsewhere.

Qianxun Intelligence is running a narrower but faster-moving version of the same playbook: a dedicated data collection team reported to be scaling toward roughly 1,000 people, more than 200,000 hours of real-world interaction data gathered so far from a mix of internet video, teleoperation, and wearable collection, and a stated goal of passing one million hours within 2026. Its fifth-generation wearable device is reported to cut data acquisition cost to roughly a tenth of earlier methods, which, if accurate, is doing a lot of the work behind that growth curve.

Neither company is alone. Trade press coverage from September 2026 counted [at least 90 dedicated embodied intelligence data collection centers](https://eu.36kr.com/en/p/3836753394021509) that had opened across China in under two years, spreading beyond first-tier cities into places like Zhengzhou, Wuxi, Jinan, and Mianyang. Paxini alone opened four new centers in Suqian, Wuhan, Zigong, and Ganzhou in that window.

## The layer almost every program underfunds: making the data actually usable

Collecting footage is not the same as producing training data, and this is where most of the real engineering effort should go, even though it attracts the least attention. A 2026 processing-pipeline survey lays out four requirements that sit between raw capture and a trainable dataset: action representation alignment across different robots and interfaces, multimodal temporal synchronization across camera, depth, and state streams, language semantic normalization so task instructions are labeled consistently, and data quality control to catch and filter degraded sessions before they reach training.

## Matching the method to what you are actually missing

The honest starting question is not "which method is best." It is "which layer of the stack am I missing for this specific task."

| If you have... | What's actually missing | Start with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A robot and a task, but zero data | A baseline to validate the approach cheaply | Pooled datasets (Open X-Embodiment) for a quick sanity check |
| Pretraining breadth but no embodiment match | Trajectories matched to your actual hardware | Teleoperation on the target robot, even in small volume |
| A good policy that only works in the lab | Environment and task diversity | Wearable or handheld capture across more sites and operators |
| Volume but no way to tell if it is good | Synchronization, calibration logging, and QA | The processing layer above, before collecting another hour |
| A narrow task that is dangerous or rare to stage | Safe coverage of edge cases | Simulation, validated against a small real-world sample |

## What this means for a team starting today

Three things worth doing before writing a single line of a data collection plan. Check whether a pooled dataset already covers a meaningful slice of your task, because validating an approach on free data is always cheaper than validating it on data you paid to collect. Decide, in writing, what share of your budget goes to each layer of the stack rather than defaulting to whichever method is easiest to start, since that default is almost always the reason programs end up volume-rich and diversity-poor. And build the synchronization, calibration, and schema discipline in from day one, because retrofitting it onto six months of inconsistent footage costs more than doing it first would have.

The teams spending the most on this problem right now, whether that is a Beijing collection center with a thousand-person workforce or a five-person robotics startup, are converging on the same answer: no single method closes the gap. The gap closes when methods are combined deliberately, measured honestly, and kept clean enough to actually train on.
