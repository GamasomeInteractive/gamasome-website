Picture a team building a peg-in-hole assembly policy. They mount a wrist camera, record a few hundred clean demonstrations in 4K, retrain twice, and still cannot explain why the policy keeps jamming the peg against the hole wall a few millimeters short. The video looks complete. It shows the peg, the hole, the approach, the contact. What it does not show is how hard the peg was pressing against the wall right before it slipped in, because nobody put a force sensor on the wrist. The camera recorded everything except the one signal the task actually depended on.

This is the quiet failure mode behind a lot of robotics data programs: treating "we have a camera recording" as equivalent to "we are capturing the data this task needs." They are not the same claim, and the gap between them is what this article is about.

## Why "we recorded video" is not the same as "we collected the data"

Most guides to sensor data collection are written for industrial IoT: temperature probes, vibration monitors, pressure gauges on a factory line, feeding a dashboard. That content answers a different question than the one a robotics team is actually asking. A [standardbots breakdown of robot sensor types](https://standardbots.com/blog/types-of-robot-sensors-how-they-work-practical-applications) is closer to the real territory: RGB cameras for semantic texture, RGB-D cameras that add per-pixel depth, and event-based cameras that react to brightness changes on microsecond timescales, each suited to a different part of what a robot needs to perceive.

The humanoid and mobile-robot sensor market is growing fast enough that getting this right has real commercial stakes. [IDTechEx forecasts the humanoid robot sensor market could exceed \$6.59 billion by 2037](https://www.electricvehiclesresearch.com/articles/35149/us-6-59b-market-opportunity-for-sensors-in-humanoid-robots-by-2037), growing at a 21.4% CAGR, and groups commercial humanoid sensing into five typical categories: perception, force-torque, encoders, IMUs, and, increasingly, tactile. That is a useful signal that the sensor stack for robots is still expanding, not settling, which is exactly why the capture side deserves its own treatment separate from what happens to the data afterward.

This article stays on the capture side on purpose. What happens once these streams exist, combining them into one model of the world, is covered in our companion piece on [sensor fusion in physical AI](/blog/sensor-fusion-in-physical-ai/). Here, the question is narrower and, we'd argue, more foundational: what does each sensor actually record, when do you need it, what does it cost you, and how do you keep it from drifting apart from every other sensor on the rig.

## The sensor selection ladder: six questions a capture rig has to answer

Every sensor on a robot exists to answer one specific question a task is asking. Ordering them by the question they answer, from the cheapest and most universal to the most precise and contact-specific, makes it obvious why real rigs stack several sensors rather than picking a favorite.

## Why the clock matters more than the sensor spec sheet

A sensor's rated accuracy means nothing if its timestamp is wrong relative to the other sensors on the rig. This is the part of sensor data collection that gets the least attention and causes the most expensive failures.

Research on robotic sensor synchronization backs this up directly. One paper on precise sensor synchronization found that [triggering cameras and IMUs from host software introduces different delays per device](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.16045), because each sensor's driver stack behaves differently, and built a custom board that sends trigger pulses to every sensor with minimal variation instead. A separate mapping-robot dataset paper describes a simpler but effective version of the same idea: a microcontroller generates trigger pulses and timestamps them, those timestamps are logged on the host, and [frames triggered together are matched to those timestamps in post-processing](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.12497), because USB and CPU scheduling otherwise make camera frames arrive at inconsistent times even when they were captured together.

A practitioner write-up on egocentric robotics capture lays out a useful hierarchy, from strongest to weakest: sensors sharing one physical clock, then hardware trigger lines or a common timestamping unit, then network time protocols like NTP or PTP as a fallback. It also makes a distinction worth repeating: [accuracy is the average offset from a reference clock, while jitter is how much that offset varies between samples](https://www.digitaldividedata.com/blog/sensor-synchronization-for-egocentric-robotics-data), and jitter is usually the harder problem to fix after the fact, because a steady bias can be corrected in post-processing but scattered, inconsistent timestamps generally cannot.

## What a real capture program actually tracks

A [case study from Defined.ai](https://defined.ai/case-study/sensor-data-collection-robotics) on sensor data collection for robotics gives a concrete sense of what "clean" means in practice, rather than in the abstract. The client needed synchronized recordings of natural human behavior including hand and body motion, gaze, depth, and grip force. Defined.ai delivered 225 hours of finished, synchronized recordings across multiple kits, held the dropped-frame rate to 1 to 2% against a 10% ceiling, and supported a minimum of 30 frames per second throughout. They also capped recording at 5 hours per kit, per environment, per task, specifically to limit duplication and force variety into the dataset rather than letting volume substitute for coverage.

None of those numbers are universal targets. They are what one team decided to measure and hold itself to. The pattern worth copying is that there were numbers at all: a frame-drop ceiling, a minimum frame rate, and a duplication cap, checked during collection rather than discovered during training.

## Matching the sensor to what the task is actually asking

| Sensor | What it actually measures | Typical cost tier | When you need it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RGB camera | Color, texture, semantics, labels | Lowest | Nearly every task; rarely sufficient alone |
| Depth / stereo camera | Short-range 3D shape | Low to mid | Manipulation, grasping, close-range navigation |
| LiDAR | Precise distance and structure at range | Highest | Outdoor or long-range mobile robots, vehicles |
| IMU | Acceleration and rotation, fast | Low | Legged robots, drones, any fast-moving platform |
| Force-torque | Contact force and torque at a joint | Mid to high | Assembly, insertion, anything that pushes or pulls |
| Tactile | Contact location, pressure pattern, slip | Mid to high, emerging | Dexterous manipulation, deformable or fragile objects |

If a task sits mostly in free space, cameras, depth, and an IMU usually cover it. The moment a task involves sustained contact, the table's bottom two rows stop being optional. Our [3D point cloud data collection](/services/3d-point-cloud-data-collection/) and [LiDAR data collection](/services/lidar-data-collection/) services cover the range end of this table; most of what we see teams get wrong is under-investing in the contact end instead.

## What this means for a team starting today

Start from the task, not the catalog. Write down the specific questions your task needs answered, not "what sensors do good robots have," and let the sensor selection ladder above tell you which rungs you actually need. A pick-and-place task in a stable, well-lit cell probably needs rungs one, two, and four. A peg-in-hole or deformable-object task needs rungs five and six as well, and skipping them is the single most common reason a policy looks fine in review and fails in deployment.

Decide your synchronization method before you buy a single sensor, not after the first recording session. Hardware triggers and a shared clock cost very little compared to a data collection campaign, and retrofitting synchronization onto months of already-recorded, already-drifted footage is far more expensive than building it in from the first session.

Measure what you deliver, not just what you intend. A frame-drop ceiling, a minimum sample rate, and a documented calibration baseline, checked every session rather than assumed, turn "we recorded it" into "we can train on it."
