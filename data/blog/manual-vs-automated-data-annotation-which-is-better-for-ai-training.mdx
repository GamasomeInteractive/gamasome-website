[When Meta built the dataset behind its Segment Anything model, people drew the first 4.3 million masks by hand, with a model helping. By the time the project finished, \[99.1 percent of the 1.1 billion released masks had been generated fully automatically\](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02643). It is the most cited proof that automated data annotation works at a scale no human team could match.

Read the paper closely and it proves something else too. The automation only worked because humans went first. They labeled the early batches, corrected the model's drafts, and stayed in the loop until the machine was good enough to be trusted on its own. The 99.1 percent sits on top of the human-labeled part.

This article is about the decision every AI team faces before training: label the data by hand, let a model label it, or split the work. It is a close cousin of the broader question we cover in \[what data annotation is\](/blog/what-is-data-annotation/), but a sharper one. Not "what is annotation," but "where should the people go, and what happens to the labels nobody really looks at."

  \<p className="text-gray-700">Neither approach is better across the board. \<strong className="font-semibold text-gray-900">Manual annotation\</strong> is more reliable on ambiguous, novel, or high-stakes data. \<strong className="font-semibold text-gray-900">Automated annotation\</strong> is faster and far cheaper at scale on well-defined tasks. \## Manual vs automated is a spectrum, not a switch

Most comparisons frame this as a binary. In practice there are four common setups, and they differ in two things: who draws the label, and who checks it.

Every row still has a person in it somewhere. That is the point. You are never choosing between humans and machines. You are choosing where the human sits, and how much of their attention each label gets. If the difference between annotation and labeling itself is fuzzy, our breakdown of \[data annotation vs data labeling\](/blog/data-annotation-vs-data-labeling/) clears it up.

\## Where hand labeling still earns its cost

The usual case for manual annotation is "humans are more accurate." That is only half true, and the false half matters.

Researchers from MIT and Amazon audited the test sets of 10 of the most widely used computer vision, language, and audio benchmarks. They \[estimated an average label error rate of 3.3 percent\](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14749), including 2,916 wrong labels in the ImageNet validation set, about 6 percent of it. These were carefully built, human-labeled datasets used across the whole field. The mislabeled examples are \[public and browsable\](https://labelerrors.com/).

The same study found something sharper. On ImageNet with corrected labels, the smaller ResNet-18 beat ResNet-50 once the share of originally mislabeled test examples rose by just 6 percent. A few points of bad labels can flip which model you believe is best.

So manual annotation isn't valuable because people are flawless. It's valuable because people are good at three things models still struggle with. They notice when the label set itself is wrong, like a "damaged carton" class that really needs two sub-classes. They handle the first examples of anything new, such as a new SKU or a new failure mode a model has never seen. And they judge intent: whether a robot succeeded or got lucky, whether a review is sarcastic.

Manual annotation is also slow, and it barely gets cheaper with scale. The ten-thousandth label costs roughly what the first one did. That is the price of judgment.

\## Three places automation has already won

Automation crossed a line in the last few years. On some tasks it isn't just faster than people. It beats the cheapest human option outright.

\## When automation goes wrong, it goes wrong the same way every time

Here is what that looks like in practice. The scenario below is a composite built from patterns we see across annotation programs, not a single named customer.

A perception team at a warehouse robotics startup needed 40,000 segmentation masks of pallets, totes, and cartons before a customer pilot. Hand labeling would take two months. A foundation segmentation model could pre-label the whole set over a weekend. They chose the model, reviewers cleared the queue in nine days, and the acceptance rate came in at 97 percent.

Three weeks into the pilot, the robot started misjudging grasp points on shrink-wrapped pallets. Glare off the plastic wrap had fooled the pre-labeler into drawing masks a few centimeters too wide, on almost every wrapped pallet. Reviewers, moving fast through thousands of almost-right outlines, had approved them.

Hand labeling would have produced errors too. But tired people make different mistakes on different images. This model made the same mistake on the same kind of image thousands of times, and the robot learned it perfectly.

!\[Error patterns in manual, automated, and hybrid data annotation: scattered, clustered, and rubber-stamped label errors\](/uploads/blog/annotation-error-patterns-manual-automated-hybrid.webp)

Red dots are label errors. Green checks are reviewer approvals. Illustrative, not measured data.

Random errors from people tend to wash out in training, as long as there aren't too many. The model sees the right label far more often than any one wrong version. Systematic errors don't wash out. They appear every time the same condition shows up, whether that's glare, a rare object, a camera angle, or a dialect, and the model treats them as a real pattern.

Nearly half, 45.3 percent, had hit two or more in a single project. A systematic labeling error is a textbook cascade. It looks fine in review and fails in deployment.

\## The cost nobody budgets for: review

Once a model drafts the labels, your spend moves from labeling to reviewing. Most cost comparisons stop there and call it savings. But reviewing is a different job psychologically, and it fails in a predictable way.

A 2026 study in the \[Harvard Data Science Review\](https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/nrcn4h7d) ran a randomized experiment with 2,784 people checking AI-suggested labels. When flagging an error meant typing in the corrected value, reviewers engaged less and accepted more wrong suggestions. People who were more skeptical of AI turned out to be more accurate reviewers.

Put simply, extra effort to reject a label pushes people toward approving it. If your tool makes "accept" one click and "fix" ten clicks, you have built a rubber-stamp machine.

    \<li>COCO-style manual masks at about 91 seconds each: \<strong className="font-semibold text-gray-900">roughly 25,300 annotator hours\</strong>\</li>
    \<li>Model-assisted masks at 14 seconds each: \<strong className="font-semibold text-gray-900">roughly 3,900 annotator hours\</strong>\</li>
  \</ul>
  \<p className="text-\[15px\] leading-relaxed text-gray-600">That is about 21,000 hours saved. Now ask how much of the remaining 3,900 hours is real scrutiny and how much is scrolling. If a reviewer spends two seconds glancing at each mask, you didn't buy review. You bought a timestamp.\</p>
\</div>

Good review design fixes most of this. Make rejection a single action, and let the fix happen later or go to someone else. Slip known-wrong pre-labels, often called honeypots, into the queue so you can measure whether reviewers catch them. Treat a very high approval rate as a question, because 97 percent means either the model is excellent or nobody is looking, and only a golden set can tell you which. And run some batches with the model's confidence score hidden, since a "0.94" on screen invites trust.

The trap is a top-right label that looks like a top-left one. It seems easy to check on screen, so the team automates it. But confirming it really needs context the reviewer doesn't have. That's where the most expensive failures hide, and robot data is full of them.

\## Robot data plays by different rules

Most manual vs automated comparisons assume a static image and a box. Robot training data isn't that. A single teleoperated episode can include four camera streams, joint states, gripper state, and a force-torque trace across a minute of continuous motion. Our guide to \[what physical AI is\](/blog/what-is-physical-ai/) covers why this data behaves so differently.

One episode carries several layers of labels, and each layer lands in a different quadrant of the matrix.

\## Mistakes teams make before they label a single item

\<div className="not-prose my-8 grid gap-5 font-\['Poppins'\] sm\:grid-cols-2">
  \<div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6">
    \<h4 className="mb-2 text-\[16px\] font-bold text-gray-900">Buying automation before building a golden set\</h4>
    \<p className="text-\[14.5px\] leading-relaxed text-gray-600">Without a few hundred to a few thousand items labeled carefully by people and used only for measurement, there is no way to tell whether the automated labels are good. Teams that buy the tool first end up trusting a number they can't check.\</p>
  \</div>

  \<div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6">
    \<h4 className="mb-2 text-\[16px\] font-bold text-gray-900">Picking one method for the whole project\</h4>
    \<p className="text-\[14.5px\] leading-relaxed text-gray-600">"We're an automated shop" is how high-cost, hard-to-check labels get waved through alongside the easy ones. The choice belongs at the level of each label type, not the dataset.\</p>
  \</div>

  \<div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6">
    \<h4 className="mb-2 text-\[16px\] font-bold text-gray-900">Reading acceptance rate as quality\</h4>
    \<p className="text-\[14.5px\] leading-relaxed text-gray-600">A review queue that approves nearly everything looks like success on a dashboard. It is just as likely to mean nobody is really looking. Honeypot catch rate is the number that tells you which.\</p>
  \</div>

  \<div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6">
    \<h4 className="mb-2 text-\[16px\] font-bold text-gray-900">Averaging away the errors that matter\</h4>
    \<p className="text-\[14.5px\] leading-relaxed text-gray-600">A 98 percent overall accuracy can hide a 60 percent accuracy on the one condition your deployment site is full of. Slice error rates by lighting, location, object type, and operator, or systematic errors stay invisible.\</p>
  \</div>
\</div>

\<div className="not-prose my-8 rounded-xl border border-gray-200 border-l-4 border-l-\[#000B71\] bg-\[#000B71\]/\[0.04\] p-6 font-\['Poppins'\]">
  \<p className="mb-4 text-xs font-semibold tracking-\[0.08em\] text-\[#000B71\] uppercase">A field note on honesty\</p>
  \<p className="text-gray-700">Read the automation headlines carefully. The PNAS study showed ChatGPT beating crowd workers, not trained experts, whose labels were the yardstick. SAM's automatic masks were judged against human-made ones. Even the "gold standard" benchmarks carry a few percent of human error. Nobody in this debate has a perfect labeler, and any vendor implying otherwise, human or machine, deserves a closer look.\</p>
\</div>

\## If you were starting a dataset on Monday

Label the first batch by hand, in-house, even if you plan to automate later. The goal isn't saving money. It's finding out what your guidelines got wrong, because the first few hundred items almost always break the taxonomy somewhere. Turn the best of that batch into your golden set before you buy or build any automation.

Then score every label type on the Attention Budget Matrix and decide its workflow on its own terms. Staff the review queue with people who are a little suspicious of the model, give them a one-click reject, and run a honeypot batch whenever the acceptance rate creeps toward 100 percent. Budget for relabeling too. Your label definitions will change, and programmatic labels, an approach \[Snorkel AI\](https://snorkel.ai/automated-data-labeling/) and others champion, are far cheaper to regenerate than hand labels.

Who does this work is a separate decision from how it gets done. We cover that side in \[in-house vs outsourced data annotation\](/blog/in-house-vs-outsourced-data-annotation/), and if rare scenarios are your biggest labeling headache, our piece on \[edge case data collection\](/blog/edge-case-data-collection/) covers how to find them in the first place. The bigger shift is already underway: annotators are moving from drawing labels to defining, auditing, and adjudicating them. Fewer people will trace polygons. More will decide what a "successful grasp" means and prove the machine agrees.

They are the ones that cause the most expensive failures.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## When automation goes wrong, it goes wrong the same way every time&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Here is what that looks like in practice. The scenario below is a composite built from patterns we see across annotation programs, not a single named customer.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;A perception team at a warehouse robotics startup needed 40,000 segmentation masks of pallets, totes, and cartons before a customer pilot. Hand labeling would take two months. A foundation segmentation model could pre-label the whole set over a weekend. They chose the model, reviewers cleared the queue in nine days, and the acceptance rate came in at 97 percent.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Three weeks into the pilot, the robot started misjudging grasp points on shrink-wrapped pallets. Glare off the plastic wrap had fooled the pre-labeler into drawing masks a few centimeters too wide, on almost every wrapped pallet. Reviewers, moving fast through thousands of almost-right outlines, had approved them.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Hand labeling would have produced errors too. But tired people make different mistakes on different images. This model made the same mistake on the same kind of image thousands of times, and the robot learned it perfectly.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;!\[Error patterns in manual, automated, and hybrid data annotation: scattered, clustered, and rubber-stamped label errors](/uploads/blog/annotation-error-patterns-manual-automated-hybrid.webp)&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Red dots are label errors. Green checks are reviewer approvals. Illustrative, not measured data.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Random errors from people tend to wash out in training, as long as there aren't too many. The model sees the right label far more often than any one wrong version. Systematic errors don't wash out. They appear every time the same condition shows up, whether that's glare, a rare object, a camera angle, or a dialect, and the model treats them as a real pattern.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;\<div className="not-prose my-8 overflow-x-auto rounded-xl border border-gray-200 font-\['Poppins']"\>&#xD;&#xA;  \<figcaption className="mt-3 text-sm text-gray-500"\>Title of a \<a href="https://storage.googleapis.com/gweb-research2023-media/pubtools/5936.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener" className="text-\[#000B71] underline underline-offset-2 hover\:text-sky-600"\>2021 Google Research study\</a\> on data cascades in high-stakes AI\</figcaption\>&#xD;&#xA;\</figure\>&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;That study found that 92 percent of the AI practitioners it interviewed had hit at least one "data cascade," a data problem that surfaces as a failure much later in the pipeline. Nearly half, 45.3 percent, had hit two or more in a single project. A systematic labeling error is a textbook cascade. It looks fine in review and fails in deployment.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## The cost nobody budgets for: review&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Once a model drafts the labels, your spend moves from labeling to reviewing. Most cost comparisons stop there and call it savings. But reviewing is a different job psychologically, and it fails in a predictable way.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;A 2026 study in the \[Harvard Data Science Review](https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/nrcn4h7d) ran a randomized experiment with 2,784 people checking AI-suggested labels. When flagging an error meant typing in the corrected value, reviewers engaged less and accepted more wrong suggestions. People who were more skeptical of AI turned out to be more accurate reviewers.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Put simply, extra effort to reject a label pushes people toward approving it. If your tool makes "accept" one click and "fix" ten clicks, you have built a rubber-stamp machine.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;\<div className="not-prose my-8 rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6 font-\['Poppins']"\>&#xD;&#xA;  \<p className="mb-4 text-xs font-semibold tracking-\[0.08em] text-\[#000B71] uppercase"\>A quick effort calculation\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;  \<p className="mb-3 text-\[15px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600"\>Using the Segment Anything paper's figures for one million masks:\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;  \<ul className="mb-3 space-y-2 text-\[15px] text-gray-600"\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<li\>COCO-style manual masks at about 91 seconds each: \<strong className="font-semibold text-gray-900"\>roughly 25,300 annotator hours\</strong\>\</li\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<li\>Model-assisted masks at 14 seconds each: \<strong className="font-semibold text-gray-900"\>roughly 3,900 annotator hours\</strong\>\</li\>&#xD;&#xA;  \</ul\>&#xD;&#xA;  \<p className="text-\[15px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600"\>That is about 21,000 hours saved. Now ask how much of the remaining 3,900 hours is real scrutiny and how much is scrolling. If a reviewer spends two seconds glancing at each mask, you didn't buy review. You bought a timestamp.\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;\</div\>&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Good review design fixes most of this. Make rejection a single action, and let the fix happen later or go to someone else. Slip known-wrong pre-labels, often called honeypots, into the queue so you can measure whether reviewers catch them. Treat a very high approval rate as a question, because 97 percent means either the model is excellent or nobody is looking, and only a golden set can tell you which. And run some batches with the model's confidence score hidden, since a "0.94" on screen invites trust.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## The Attention Budget Matrix: decide label by label&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Here is the framework we use with teams. Instead of picking manual or automated for a whole project, score each type of label on two questions. First, how costly is a wrong label downstream? Second, how cheaply can a person verify the machine's version of it?&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;!\[Attention Budget Matrix for choosing manual or automated data annotation by error cost and verification cost](/uploads/blog/attention-budget-matrix-data-annotation.webp)&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Score each label type, not the whole project. Most datasets have labels in at least three quadrants.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;\<div className="not-prose my-8 grid gap-5 font-\['Poppins'] sm\:grid-cols-2"\>&#xD;&#xA;  \<div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6"\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<p className="mb-2 font-mono text-\[11px] tracking-\[0.06em] text-\[#000B71] uppercase"\>Low cost · cheap check\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<h4 className="mb-2 text-\[16px] font-bold text-gray-900"\>Automate and sample\</h4\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<p className="text-\[14.5px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600"\>Bounding boxes on common items in a product catalog. A wrong box rarely breaks anything, and anyone can spot one instantly. Audit a random slice of each batch, and when you find a pattern, fix the model or prompt instead of individual labels.\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;  \</div\>&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;  \<div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6"\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<p className="mb-2 font-mono text-\[11px] tracking-\[0.06em] text-\[#000B71] uppercase"\>High cost · cheap check\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<h4 className="mb-2 text-\[16px] font-bold text-gray-900"\>Pre-label, human signs off\</h4\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<p className="text-\[14.5px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600"\>Pallet and tote masks that drive a robot's grasp planning. Errors are expensive, but a trained eye catches them fast. This is where the warehouse team's labels belonged: every item reviewed, with honeypots to prove the review is real.\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;  \</div\>&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;  \<div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6"\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<p className="mb-2 font-mono text-\[11px] tracking-\[0.06em] text-\[#000B71] uppercase"\>Low cost · hard check\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<h4 className="mb-2 text-\[16px] font-bold text-gray-900"\>Automate and measure\</h4\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<p className="text-\[14.5px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600"\>Topic tags on millions of support tickets. Checking each one costs more than the occasional wrong tag. Track accuracy against a golden set, sliced by category, and accept a known, bounded noise rate.\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;  \</div\>&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;  \<div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6"\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<p className="mb-2 font-mono text-\[11px] tracking-\[0.06em] text-\[#000B71] uppercase"\>High cost · hard check\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<h4 className="mb-2 text-\[16px] font-bold text-gray-900"\>Human-led, double-annotated\</h4\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<p className="text-\[14.5px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600"\>Lesion boundaries in medical scans, or whether a robot actually completed a task. Experts label from scratch, two people per item, and disagreements go to adjudication. Models can suggest. They don't decide.\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;  \</div\>&#xD;&#xA;\</div\>&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;The trap is a top-right label that looks like a top-left one. It seems easy to check on screen, so the team automates it. But confirming it really needs context the reviewer doesn't have. That's where the most expensive failures hide, and robot data is full of them.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## Robot data plays by different rules&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Most manual vs automated comparisons assume a static image and a box. Robot training data isn't that. A single teleoperated episode can include four camera streams, joint states, gripper state, and a force-torque trace across a minute of continuous motion. Our guide to \[what physical AI is](/blog/what-is-physical-ai/) covers why this data behaves so differently.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;One episode carries several layers of labels, and each layer lands in a different quadrant of the matrix.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;!\[Robot demonstration episode annotation layers showing which labels to automate and which need human annotation](/uploads/blog/robot-episode-annotation-layers-manual-automated.webp)&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;The force dip during "transport" is the kind of signal a camera-only pre-labeler misses: the part slipped in the gripper.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;\<div className="not-prose my-8 overflow-x-auto rounded-xl border border-gray-200 font-\['Poppins']"\>&#xD;&#xA;  \<table className="w-full min-w-\[620px] text-left text-\[14.5px]"\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<thead className="bg-gray-50"\>&#xD;&#xA;      \<tr\>&#xD;&#xA;        \<th className="px-4 py-3 font-mono text-xs tracking-\[0.06em] text-\[#000B71] uppercase"\>Label layer\</th\>&#xD;&#xA;        \<th className="px-4 py-3 font-mono text-xs tracking-\[0.06em] text-\[#000B71] uppercase"\>Recommended approach\</th\>&#xD;&#xA;        \<th className="px-4 py-3 font-mono text-xs tracking-\[0.06em] text-\[#000B71] uppercase"\>Why\</th\>&#xD;&#xA;      \</tr\>&#xD;&#xA;    \</thead\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<tbody className="divide-y divide-gray-200 text-gray-600"\>&#xD;&#xA;      \<tr\>\<td className="px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-gray-900"\>Camera calibration and sync\</td\>\<td className="px-4 py-3"\>Fully automated\</td\>\<td className="px-4 py-3"\>It's math, not judgment. Thresholds catch drift reliably.\</td\>\</tr\>&#xD;&#xA;      \<tr\>\<td className="px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-gray-900"\>Object masks and tracking\</td\>\<td className="px-4 py-3"\>Model-assisted, human sign-off\</td\>\<td className="px-4 py-3"\>Foundation segmenters do this well, and errors are visible at a glance.\</td\>\</tr\>&#xD;&#xA;      \<tr\>\<td className="px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-gray-900"\>Trajectory quality\</td\>\<td className="px-4 py-3"\>Automated metrics, human review of flagged episodes\</td\>\<td className="px-4 py-3"\>Jerk and oscillation are measurable. Whether a correction was intentional isn't.\</td\>\</tr\>&#xD;&#xA;      \<tr\>\<td className="px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-gray-900"\>Language instruction\</td\>\<td className="px-4 py-3"\>Vision-language model drafts, human verifies\</td\>\<td className="px-4 py-3"\>DIAL shows this scales. A wrong instruction still teaches the wrong behavior.\</td\>\</tr\>&#xD;&#xA;      \<tr\>\<td className="px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-gray-900"\>Action segment boundaries\</td\>\<td className="px-4 py-3"\>Human-led, model-suggested\</td\>\<td className="px-4 py-3"\>When a grasp "starts" depends on contact, which lives in the force data.\</td\>\</tr\>&#xD;&#xA;      \<tr\>\<td className="px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-gray-900"\>Task success and failure type\</td\>\<td className="px-4 py-3"\>Human, ideally double-labeled\</td\>\<td className="px-4 py-3"\>On camera, a success and a near-miss can look identical.\</td\>\</tr\>&#xD;&#xA;    \</tbody\>&#xD;&#xA;  \</table\>&#xD;&#xA;\</div\>&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Across our \[physical AI data collection](/services/physical-ai-data-collection/) programs, the label teams automate first is often the one that should be automated last: whether the episode succeeded. A gripper closing around a part looks like a win on video. The force trace tells a different story. The part slipped, the operator recovered, and the "success" is really a recovery demonstration.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;\<div className="not-prose my-8 rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6 font-\['Poppins']"\>&#xD;&#xA;  \<p className="mb-4 text-xs font-semibold tracking-\[0.08em] text-\[#000B71] uppercase"\>From the field\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;  \<div className="grid gap-3 sm\:grid-cols-\[110px_1fr]"\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<span className="font-mono text-xs tracking-\[0.06em] text-\[#000B71] uppercase"\>Situation\</span\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<p className="text-\[15px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600"\>A team collects bimanual pick-and-place demonstrations and has a vision-language model tag each episode "success" or "fail" from the overhead camera.\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<span className="font-mono text-xs tracking-\[0.06em] text-\[#000B71] uppercase"\>Problem\</span\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<p className="text-\[15px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600"\>Slips and regrasp recoveries get tagged as clean successes. Automate the checks that are cheap to verify, and put people on the ones that aren't. We go deeper on that in \[physical AI data quality](/blog/physical-ai-data-quality/) and in our guide to \[human demonstrations for robot learning](/blog/human-demonstrations-physical-ai/).&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Research is heading the same way. A 2026 NeurIPS paper, DeMiAn, used an automatic vision-language pipeline to \[re-label more than 1 million robot manipulation episodes and 50,000 egocentric human videos](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/poster/150612) with richer descriptions. Without collecting a single new demonstration, its learned instructor raised RoboCasa task success by 5 points. Automated annotation is becoming a real lever for robot learning, as long as it sits on top of demonstrations people captured and verified well.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## Mistakes teams make before they label a single item&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;\<div className="not-prose my-8 grid gap-5 font-\['Poppins'] sm\:grid-cols-2"\>&#xD;&#xA;  \<div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6"\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<h4 className="mb-2 text-\[16px] font-bold text-gray-900"\>Buying automation before building a golden set\</h4\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<p className="text-\[14.5px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600"\>Without a few hundred to a few thousand items labeled carefully by people and used only for measurement, there is no way to tell whether the automated labels are good. Teams that buy the tool first end up trusting a number they can't check.\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;  \</div\>&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;  \<div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6"\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<h4 className="mb-2 text-\[16px] font-bold text-gray-900"\>Picking one method for the whole project\</h4\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<p className="text-\[14.5px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600"\>"We're an automated shop" is how high-cost, hard-to-check labels get waved through alongside the easy ones. The choice belongs at the level of each label type, not the dataset.\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;  \</div\>&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;  \<div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6"\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<h4 className="mb-2 text-\[16px] font-bold text-gray-900"\>Reading acceptance rate as quality\</h4\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<p className="text-\[14.5px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600"\>A review queue that approves nearly everything looks like success on a dashboard. It is just as likely to mean nobody is really looking. Honeypot catch rate is the number that tells you which.\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;  \</div\>&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;  \<div className="rounded-xl border border-gray-200 bg-gray-50 p-6"\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<h4 className="mb-2 text-\[16px] font-bold text-gray-900"\>Averaging away the errors that matter\</h4\>&#xD;&#xA;    \<p className="text-\[14.5px] leading-relaxed text-gray-600"\>A 98 percent overall accuracy can hide a 60 percent accuracy on the one condition your deployment site is full of. Slice error rates by lighting, location, object type, and operator, or systematic errors stay invisible.\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;  \</div\>&#xD;&#xA;\</div\>&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;\<div className="not-prose my-8 rounded-xl border border-gray-200 border-l-4 border-l-\[#000B71] bg-\[#000B71]/\[0.04] p-6 font-\['Poppins']"\>&#xD;&#xA;  \<p className="mb-4 text-xs font-semibold tracking-\[0.08em] text-\[#000B71] uppercase"\>A field note on honesty\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;  \<p className="text-gray-700"\>Read the automation headlines carefully. The PNAS study showed ChatGPT beating crowd workers, not trained experts, whose labels were the yardstick. SAM's automatic masks were judged against human-made ones. Even the "gold standard" benchmarks carry a few percent of human error. Nobody in this debate has a perfect labeler, and any vendor implying otherwise, human or machine, deserves a closer look.\</p\>&#xD;&#xA;\</div\>&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## If you were starting a dataset on Monday&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Label the first batch by hand, in-house, even if you plan to automate later. The goal isn't saving money. It's finding out what your guidelines got wrong, because the first few hundred items almost always break the taxonomy somewhere. Turn the best of that batch into your golden set before you buy or build any automation.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Then score every label type on the Attention Budget Matrix and decide its workflow on its own terms. Staff the review queue with people who are a little suspicious of the model, give them a one-click reject, and run a honeypot batch whenever the acceptance rate creeps toward 100 percent. Budget for relabeling too. Your label definitions will change, and programmatic labels, an approach \[Snorkel AI](https://snorkel.ai/automated-data-labeling/) and others champion, are far cheaper to regenerate than hand labels.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Who does this work is a separate decision from how it gets done. The bigger shift is already underway: annotators are moving from drawing labels to defining, auditing, and adjudicating them. Fewer people will trace polygons.
