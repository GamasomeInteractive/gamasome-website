\<section class="answer-box" aria-labelledby="direct-answer">
  \<h2 id="direct-answer">The short answer\</h2>
  \<p>Neither approach is better across the board. \<strong>Manual annotation\</strong> is more reliable on ambiguous, novel, or high-stakes data. \<strong>Automated annotation\</strong> is faster and far cheaper at scale on well-defined tasks. Most production teams now run a hybrid: models draft labels, and humans verify the ones where a mistake would be costly or hard to catch.\</p>
  \<ul class="takeaways">
    \<li>Automation doesn't remove human labeling. It multiplies a small, carefully human-labeled seed.\</li>
    \<li>Manual errors are scattered. Automated errors repeat. Models learn repeated errors very well.\</li>
    \<li>The real cost of automation is review. Badly designed review turns humans into rubber stamps.\</li>
    \<li>Decide per label type, not per project. Use the Attention Budget Matrix below.\</li>
  \</ul>
\</section>

\<h2 id="story">The 97% acceptance rate that should have been a warning\</h2>

\<div class="story">
  \<span class="tag-composite">Composite scenario, built from patterns we see across annotation programs\</span>
  \<p>Priya leads perception at a warehouse robotics startup. Her team needed 40,000 segmentation masks of pallets, totes, and cartons before a pilot with a logistics customer. Hand-labeling would take two months. A foundation segmentation model could pre-label everything over a weekend.\</p>
  \<p>They chose the model. Reviewers cleared the queue in nine days. The acceptance rate was 97%. The team celebrated.\</p>
  \<p>Three weeks into the pilot, the robot started misjudging grasp points on shrink-wrapped pallets. Glare off the plastic wrap had fooled the pre-labeler. It drew masks a few centimeters too wide on almost every wrapped pallet. The reviewers, moving fast through thousands of almost-right outlines, approved them.\</p>
  \<p>Here's the uncomfortable part. Hand labeling would have produced errors too. But tired humans make different mistakes on different images. This model made the same mistake on the same kind of image thousands of times. And the robot learned it perfectly.\</p>
\</div>

\<p>That story is why "manual or automated?" is the wrong question. The useful question is: \<strong>where does human attention go, and what happens to the labels nobody really looks at?\</strong> The rest of this guide answers that.\</p>

\<h2 id="spectrum">Manual vs automated is really a spectrum\</h2>

\<p>Most comparisons treat this as a binary. In practice, there are four common setups. They differ in who draws the label and who checks it.\</p>

\<p>Notice that every row still has a human somewhere. The approaches differ in \<em>where\</em> that human sits. That's the decision you're really making. If you want the basics of the work itself first, start with our explainer on \<a href="https://www.gamasome.com/blog/what-is-data-annotation/">what data annotation is\</a>, or how it differs from \<a href="https://www.gamasome.com/blog/data-annotation-vs-data-labeling/">data labeling\</a>.\</p>

\<h2 id="manual">Where manual annotation still earns its cost\</h2>

\<p>The common pitch for manual annotation is "humans are more accurate." That's only half true, and the half that's false matters.\</p>

\<p>Researchers from MIT and Amazon audited the test sets of 10 of the most widely used computer vision, language, and audio benchmarks. They \<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14749" target="\_blank" rel="noopener">estimated an average label error rate of 3.3%\</a>, including 2,916 errors in the ImageNet validation set, about 6% of it. These were carefully built, human-labeled datasets used by the entire field. You can \<a href="https://labelerrors.com/" target="\_blank" rel="noopener">browse the mislabeled examples yourself\</a>.\</p>

\<p>The same study found something sharper. On ImageNet with corrected labels, the smaller ResNet-18 outperformed ResNet-50 once the share of originally mislabeled test examples rose by just 6%. In plain terms, a few percent of bad labels can flip which model you think is best.\</p>

\<div class="callout">
  \<span class="label">So what?\</span>
  \<p>Manual annotation isn't valuable because humans are flawless. It's valuable because humans are good at three things models still struggle with:\</p>
  \<ul>
    \<li>\<strong>Spotting that the label set itself is wrong.\</strong> A person notices that "damaged carton" needs two sub-classes. A model just picks one.\</li>
    \<li>\<strong>Handling the first examples of anything.\</strong> A new SKU, a new tool, a new failure mode. Models can't pre-label what they've never seen.\</li>
    \<li>\<strong>Judging intent.\</strong> Did the robot succeed, or did it get lucky? Is this review sarcastic? That's judgment, not pattern matching.\</li>
  \</ul>
\</div>

\<p>Manual annotation is slow and it doesn't get much cheaper with scale. Each new label costs roughly what the last one did. That's the trade you accept for judgment.\</p>

\<h2 id="automated">Where automated annotation has quietly won\</h2>

\<p>Automation has crossed a line in the last few years. On some tasks, it's not just faster. It's better than the cheap human alternative. Three public examples show how.\</p>

\<h3>Segmentation: Meta's Segment Anything data engine\</h3>
\<p>To build the SA-1B dataset, \<a href="https://ai.meta.com/" target="\_blank" rel="noopener">Meta\</a> ran a three-stage data engine. Humans started with model-assisted labeling, and \<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02643" target="\_blank" rel="noopener">average time per mask fell from 34 to 14 seconds\</a> as the model improved. The authors report that 14 seconds is 6.5x faster than COCO mask annotation. By the end, 99.1% of the 1.1 billion released masks were generated fully automatically.\</p>

\<h3>Text: LLMs vs crowd workers\</h3>
\<p>A study in \<a href="https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2305016120" target="\_blank" rel="noopener">PNAS\</a> compared \<a href="https://openai.com/" target="\_blank" rel="noopener">OpenAI's\</a> ChatGPT against crowd workers on \<a href="https://www.mturk.com/" target="\_blank" rel="noopener">Amazon Mechanical Turk\</a> across 6,183 tweets and news articles. ChatGPT's zero-shot accuracy beat the crowd workers by about 25 percentage points on average. Each label cost under $0.003, about thirty times cheaper.\</p>

\<h3>Robotics: relabeling robot demonstrations with a vision-language model\</h3>
\<p>A \<a href="https://research.google/" target="\_blank" rel="noopener">Google\</a> robotics team had 80,000 robot demonstrations, but \<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11736" target="\_blank" rel="noopener">96.5% of them had no crowd-sourced language labels\</a>. Their method, DIAL, fine-tuned a vision-language model on the small labeled slice, then used it to write instructions for the rest. Policies trained on the augmented data generalized to 60 new instructions that weren't in the original set.\</p>

\<p class="pull">Look closely and every one of these wins starts with human labels. \<span>Automation doesn't replace manual annotation. It spends it.\</span>\</p>

\<p>SAM's first stage was human-led. The PNAS study measured accuracy against labels from trained research assistants. DIAL needed 2,800 human-annotated episodes before the model could label the other 77,000 or so. The quality of your automated labels is capped by the quality of the human seed you train and test them against.\</p>

\<p>The market reflects how fast this shift is moving. \<a href="https://www.grandviewresearch.com/press-release/global-data-annotation-tools-market" target="\_blank" rel="noopener">Grand View Research projects\</a> the data annotation tools market will reach $5.33 billion by 2030, growing about 26% a year. From what we see in the market, the fastest-moving tools blend automation with human review rather than trying to remove people.\</p>

Nearly half (45.3%) had hit two or more in a single project. Systematic labeling errors are a textbook cascade. They look fine in review and fail in deployment.\</p>

\<h2 id="review">The hidden cost of automated annotation: review\</h2>

\<p>Here's the line item most cost comparisons leave out. Once a model drafts the labels, your spend shifts from labeling to reviewing. And reviewing is a psychologically different job.\</p>

\<p>A 2026 study in the \<a href="https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/nrcn4h7d" target="\_blank" rel="noopener">Harvard Data Science Review\</a> ran a randomized experiment with 2,784 people reviewing AI-suggested labels. Two findings stand out. When flagging an error required typing the corrected value, reviewers engaged less and accepted more wrong suggestions. And people who were more skeptical of AI were more accurate reviewers.\</p>

\<p>In other words, the extra effort to reject a label pushes people toward approving it. If your tool makes "accept" one click and "fix" ten clicks, you've built a rubber-stamp machine.\</p>

\<div class="callout">
  \<span class="label">A quick effort calculation\</span>
  \<p>Using the Segment Anything paper's numbers for one million masks:\</p>
  \<ul>
    \<li>COCO-style manual masks at about 91 seconds each: \<strong>roughly 25,300 annotator hours\</strong>\</li>
    \<li>Model-assisted masks at 14 seconds each: \<strong>roughly 3,900 annotator hours\</strong>\</li>
  \</ul>
  \<p>That's about 21,000 hours saved. Now ask what share of those 3,900 hours is real scrutiny versus scrolling. If reviewers spend two seconds glancing at a mask, you didn't buy review. You bought a timestamp.\</p>
\</div>

\<p>Good review design fixes most of this:\</p>
\<ul>
  \<li>\<strong>Make rejection cheap.\</strong> A reviewer should be able to flag a bad draft in one action and fix it later, or route it to someone else.\</li>
  \<li>\<strong>Seed honeypots.\</strong> Slip known-wrong pre-labels into the queue. If reviewers approve them, your review isn't working.\</li>
  \<li>\<strong>Treat high acceptance as a question.\</strong> A 97% approval rate means either the model is excellent or nobody is looking. You need a golden set to tell which.\</li>
  \<li>\<strong>Hide confidence scores sometimes.\</strong> Showing "0.94" invites trust. Blind batches keep reviewers honest.\</li>
\</ul>

    \<h4>Automate and measure\</h4>
    \<p>Example: topic tags on millions of support tickets. Checking each one costs more than the occasional error.\</p>
    \<p>Track accuracy against a golden set, sliced by category, and accept a bounded noise rate.\</p>
  \</div>
  \<div class="card">
    \<span class="q">HIGH COST · HARD CHECK\</span>
    \<h4>Human-led, double-annotated\</h4>
    \<p>Example: lesion boundaries in medical scans, or whether a robot actually completed a task.\</p>
    \<p>Experts label from scratch. Two people per item. Disagreements go to adjudication.\</p>
  \</div>
\</div>

\<p>The trap is the top-right quadrant disguised as the top-left one. A label \<em>looks\</em> easy to check on screen, so the team automates it. But actually confirming it needs context the reviewer doesn't have. That's where the most expensive failures hide, and it leads straight into robot data.\</p>

\<h2 id="robotics">Robot and physical AI data: a different rulebook\</h2>

\<p>Most manual vs automated comparisons assume a static image and a box. Robot training data isn't that. A single teleoperated episode might include four camera streams, joint states, gripper state, and a force-torque trace across 60 seconds of continuous motion. If you're new to this data type, our guide on \<a href="https://www.gamasome.com/blog/what-is-physical-ai/">what physical AI is\</a> covers why it behaves so differently.\</p>

\<h3>What we see in the field\</h3>
\<p>Across our \<a href="https://www.gamasome.com/physical-ai-data-collection">physical AI data collection programs\</a>, the label teams automate first is often the one that should be automated last: whether the episode succeeded. On video, a gripper closing around a part looks like a win. The force trace tells a different story. The part slipped, the operator recovered, and the "success" is really a recovery demonstration.\</p>

\<dl class="sposo">
  \<dt>Situation\</dt>\<dd>A team collects bimanual pick-and-place demonstrations and uses a vision-language model to tag each episode "success" or "fail" from the overhead camera.\</dd>
  \<dt>Problem\</dt>\<dd>Slips and regrasp recoveries get tagged as clean successes. The policy learns that a fumble-and-recover is the normal way to do the task.\</dd>
  \<dt>Solution\</dt>\<dd>Keep the model's tag as a suggestion. A trained reviewer confirms success using synchronized video and force data, and labels recoveries as their own category.\</dd>
  \<dt>Outcome\</dt>\<dd>Recoveries become useful training signal instead of noise, and the "clean success" set means what it says.\</dd>
\</dl>

\<p>This is also why we score every delivered episode on task success, trajectory smoothness, calibration drift, annotation consistency, and environment coverage before it reaches a client. It's the same idea as the matrix: automate the checks that are cheap to verify, and put people on the ones that aren't. More on that in our post on \<a href="https://www.gamasome.com/blog/physical-ai-data-quality/">physical AI data quality\</a> and on \<a href="https://www.gamasome.com/blog/human-demonstrations-physical-ai/">human demonstrations for robot learning\</a>.\</p>

\<p>The research direction points the same way. A 2026 NeurIPS paper, DeMiAn, used an automatic VLM pipeline to \<a href="https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/poster/150612" target="\_blank" rel="noopener">re-label over 1 million robot manipulation episodes and 50,000 egocentric human videos\</a> with richer descriptions. With no new demonstrations collected, its learned instructor raised RoboCasa task success by 5 points. Automated annotation is becoming a real lever for robot learning. It works best on top of demonstrations that humans captured and verified well.\</p>

\<h2 id="decide">Seven questions to ask before you choose\</h2>

\<p>Run through these before committing budget to either approach. If you answer "no" or "not sure" to several, start more manual than you planned.\</p>

\<ol class="check">
  \<li>\<strong>Do we have a golden set?\</strong> A few hundred to a few thousand items labeled carefully by people, used only for measuring. Without it, you can't tell whether automation is working.\</li>
  \<li>\<strong>Has a pre-labeling model seen this kind of data before?\</strong> New sensors, new sites, and new object types push it out of its comfort zone.\</li>
  \<li>\<strong>What does one wrong label cost downstream?\</strong> A misranked search result is not a robot dropping a part.\</li>
  \<li>\<strong>Can a reviewer verify a label in seconds, with what's on screen?\</strong> If they need extra context, it's a hard-check label.\</li>
  \<li>\<strong>Is our label set stable?\</strong> If the taxonomy will change twice next quarter, programmatic or prompt-based labels are easier to regenerate than hand labels.\</li>
  \<li>\<strong>Can we slice errors by condition?\</strong> Lighting, location, object type, operator. Systematic errors hide in averages.\</li>
  \<li>\<strong>Who owns review quality?\</strong> Someone needs to track honeypot catch rates and acceptance rates, not just throughput.\</li>
\</ol>

\<p>Deciding \<em>who\</em> does this work is a separate call from \<em>how\</em> it's done. We cover that in \<a href="https://www.gamasome.com/blog/in-house-vs-outsourced-data-annotation/">in-house vs outsourced data annotation\</a>.\</p>

\<h2 id="perspective">Expert perspective: what we'd do if we started a dataset Monday\</h2>

\<p>These are opinions, formed from running capture and annotation programs on real robots and real sites.\</p>

\<ul>
  \<li>\<strong>Label the first batch by hand, in-house.\</strong> Not to save money. To discover what your guidelines got wrong. The first few hundred items always break the taxonomy somewhere.\</li>
  \<li>\<strong>Build the golden set before you buy automation.\</strong> Teams do it in the opposite order, then have no way to measure what they bought.\</li>
  \<li>\<strong>Choose automation per label layer, never per project.\</strong> "We're an automated shop" is how high-cost, hard-to-check labels get waved through.\</li>
  \<li>\<strong>Hire skeptics as reviewers.\</strong> The HDSR study backs up what we see: people who distrust the model catch more of its mistakes.\</li>
  \<li>\<strong>Watch acceptance rate like a smoke alarm.\</strong> When it creeps toward 100%, run a honeypot batch that week.\</li>
  \<li>\<strong>Budget for relabeling.\</strong> Your label definitions will change. Programmatic labels, which \<a href="https://snorkel.ai/automated-data-labeling/" target="\_blank" rel="noopener">Snorkel AI\</a> and others champion, are cheap to regenerate. Hand labels aren't. Plan for both.\</li>
\</ul>

\<p>Where is this heading? Human annotators are moving from drawing labels to defining, auditing, and adjudicating them. Fewer people will draw polygons. More people will decide what a "successful grasp" means and prove that the machine agrees. That work is harder to automate, and it's where dataset quality is now decided.\</p>

\<h2 id="faq">Frequently asked questions\</h2>

\<div class="faq">
  \<details open>
    \<summary>Is manual or automated data annotation better for AI training?\</summary>
    \<p>Neither wins across the board. Manual annotation is more reliable on ambiguous, novel, or high-stakes data. Automated annotation is faster and much cheaper at scale on well-defined tasks. Most production teams use a hybrid where models draft labels and humans verify the ones where a mistake would be costly or hard to spot.\</p>
  \</details>
  \<details>
    \<summary>Is manual annotation always more accurate than automated annotation?\</summary>
    \<p>No. A study of 10 widely used benchmark datasets estimated that about 3.3% of human-made test labels were wrong on average, including roughly 6% of the ImageNet validation set. Human labels are usually better on hard cases, but they still need consensus checks and audits.\</p>
  \</details>
  \<details>
    \<summary>How much cheaper is automated data annotation?\</summary>
    \<p>It depends on the task. A PNAS study found ChatGPT text labels cost under $0.003 each, about thirty times less than Amazon Mechanical Turk. Meta's Segment Anything data engine cut mask annotation time to 14 seconds, about 6.5 times faster than COCO-style manual masks. Review and audit time still has to be budgeted.\</p>
  \</details>
  \<details>
    \<summary>What is human-in-the-loop data annotation?\</summary>
    \<p>Human-in-the-loop annotation is a workflow where a model produces draft labels and people review, correct, or reject them. The corrections are fed back to improve the model. It works well when reviewers are trained to challenge the machine instead of approving its output by default.\</p>
  \</details>
  \<details>
    \<summary>When should you avoid automated data labeling?\</summary>
    \<p>Avoid relying on automated labels when a wrong label is costly and also expensive to verify, such as medical findings, safety-critical edge cases, new object classes the model has never seen, or task-success judgments in robot demonstrations that require watching the full episode with sensor data.\</p>
  \</details>
  \<details>
    \<summary>Can large language models and vision-language models replace human annotators?\</summary>
    \<p>They can replace a large share of routine labeling, especially text classification and captioning. They do not replace the humans who define the label set, build the golden reference data, and audit for systematic errors. Every major automated labeling success to date started from a human-labeled seed.\</p>
  \</details>
  \<details>
    \<summary>How do you check the quality of automated labels?\</summary>
    \<p>Compare them against a human-built golden set, slice error rates by data condition such as lighting or object type, seed known-wrong pre-labels to test whether reviewers catch them, and treat a very high review acceptance rate as a warning sign rather than a success metric.\</p>
  \</details>
  \<details>
    \<summary>Which annotation approach works best for robotics and physical AI data?\</summary>
    \<p>Robot episodes need a layered approach. Object masks, tracking, and calibration checks automate well. Language instructions can be drafted by a vision-language model and verified by people. Action boundaries, task success, and failure types should stay human-led because they depend on video, force data, and task intent together.\</p>
  \</details>
\</div>

\<h2 id="ending">Back to the shrink-wrapped pallets\</h2>

\<p>Priya's team didn't go back to hand-labeling everything. That would have thrown away the large majority of masks the model got right. Instead, they sliced errors by condition, found the glare cluster, and moved wrapped pallets into the "pre-label, human signs off" quadrant with honeypots in the queue. Everything else stayed automated with sample audits.\</p>

\<p>The fix took a week. The lesson took longer to sink in: the model wasn't the weak point. The review design was. So when someone asks whether manual or automated annotation is better, the honest answer is a second question. Where are you putting your people, and how do you know they're really looking?\</p>

 \<h2 id="direct-answer"\>The short answer\</h2\>&#xA;  \<p\>Neither approach is better across the board. \<strong\>Manual annotation\</strong\> is more reliable on ambiguous, novel, or high-stakes data. \<strong\>Automated annotation\</strong\> is faster and far cheaper at scale on well-defined tasks. It multiplies a small, carefully human-labeled seed.\</li\>&#xA;    \<li\>Manual errors are scattered. Automated errors repeat. Her team needed 40,000 segmentation masks of pallets, totes, and cartons before a pilot with a logistics customer. Hand-labeling would take two months. A foundation segmentation model could pre-label everything over a weekend.\</p\>&#xA;  \<p\>They chose the model. Reviewers cleared the queue in nine days. The acceptance rate was 97%. The team celebrated.\</p\>&#xA;  \<p\>Three weeks into the pilot, the robot started misjudging grasp points on shrink-wrapped pallets. Glare off the plastic wrap had fooled the pre-labeler. It drew masks a few centimeters too wide on almost every wrapped pallet. The reviewers, moving fast through thousands of almost-right outlines, approved them.\</p\>&#xA;  \<p\>Here's the uncomfortable part. Hand labeling would have produced errors too. But tired humans make different mistakes on different images. This model made the same mistake on the same kind of image thousands of times. And the robot learned it perfectly.\</p\>&#xA;\</div\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>That story is why "manual or automated?" is the wrong question. The useful question is: \<strong\>where does human attention go, and what happens to the labels nobody really looks at?\</strong\> The rest of this guide answers that.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<h2 id="spectrum"\>Manual vs automated is really a spectrum\</h2\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>Most comparisons treat this as a binary. In practice, there are four common setups. They differ in who draws the label and who checks it.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<div class="table-scroll"\>&#xA;\<table\>&#xA;  \<thead\>&#xA;    \<tr\>\<th\>Approach\</th\>\<th\>Who draws the label\</th\>\<th\>Who checks it\</th\>\<th\>Typical fit\</th\>\</tr\>&#xA;  \</thead\>&#xA;  \<tbody\>&#xA;    \<tr\>\<td\>\<strong\>Manual from scratch\</strong\>\</td\>\<td\>A trained person\</td\>\<td\>A second annotator or QA sample\</td\>\<td\>New classes, ambiguous data, medical and safety-critical cases\</td\>\</tr\>&#xA;    \<tr\>\<td\>\<strong\>Model-assisted (pre-labeling)\</strong\>\</td\>\<td\>A model drafts, a person corrects\</td\>\<td\>The same person, item by item\</td\>\<td\>Segmentation, bounding boxes, object tracking\</td\>\</tr\>&#xA;    \<tr\>\<td\>\<strong\>Programmatic (weak supervision)\</strong\>\</td\>\<td\>Rules written by domain experts\</td\>\<td\>Statistical checks against a golden set\</td\>\<td\>Large text pools, document classification\</td\>\</tr\>&#xA;    \<tr\>\<td\>\<strong\>Fully automated\</strong\>\</td\>\<td\>A foundation model or LLM\</td\>\<td\>Sampled audits\</td\>\<td\>Common object classes, captions, relabeling old archives\</td\>\</tr\>&#xA;  \</tbody\>&#xA;\</table\>&#xA;\</div\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>Notice that every row still has a human somewhere. The approaches differ in \<em\>where\</em\> that human sits. That's the decision you're really making. If you want the basics of the work itself first, start with our explainer on \<a href="https://www.gamasome.com/blog/what-is-data-annotation/"\>what data annotation is\</a\>, or how it differs from \<a href="https://www.gamasome.com/blog/data-annotation-vs-data-labeling/"\>data labeling\</a\>.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<h2 id="manual"\>Where manual annotation still earns its cost\</h2\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>The common pitch for manual annotation is "humans are more accurate." That's only half true, and the half that's false matters.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>Researchers from MIT and Amazon audited the test sets of 10 of the most widely used computer vision, language, and audio benchmarks. They \<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14749" target="_blank" rel="noopener"\>estimated an average label error rate of 3.3%\</a\>, including 2,916 errors in the ImageNet validation set, about 6% of it. These were carefully built, human-labeled datasets used by the entire field. You can \<a href="https://labelerrors.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener"\>browse the mislabeled examples yourself\</a\>.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>The same study found something sharper. On ImageNet with corrected labels, the smaller ResNet-18 outperformed ResNet-50 once the share of originally mislabeled test examples rose by just 6%. In plain terms, a few percent of bad labels can flip which model you think is best.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<div class="callout"\>&#xA;  \<span class="label"\>So what?\</span\>&#xA;  \<p\>Manual annotation isn't valuable because humans are flawless. It's valuable because humans are good at three things models still struggle with:\</p\>&#xA;  \<ul\>&#xA;    \<li\>\<strong\>Spotting that the label set itself is wrong.\</strong\> A person notices that "damaged carton" needs two sub-classes. A model just picks one.\</li\>&#xA;    \<li\>\<strong\>Handling the first examples of anything.\</strong\> A new SKU, a new tool, a new failure mode. Models can't pre-label what they've never seen.\</li\>&#xA;    \<li\>\<strong\>Judging intent.\</strong\> Did the robot succeed, or did it get lucky? Is this review sarcastic? That's judgment, not pattern matching.\</li\>&#xA;  \</ul\>&#xA;\</div\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>Manual annotation is slow and it doesn't get much cheaper with scale. Each new label costs roughly what the last one did. That's the trade you accept for judgment.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<h2 id="automated"\>Where automated annotation has quietly won\</h2\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>Automation has crossed a line in the last few years. On some tasks, it's not just faster. It's better than the cheap human alternative. Three public examples show how.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<h3\>Segmentation: Meta's Segment Anything data engine\</h3\>&#xA;\<p\>To build the SA-1B dataset, \<a href="https://ai.meta.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener"\>Meta\</a\> ran a three-stage data engine. Humans started with model-assisted labeling, and \<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02643" target="_blank" rel="noopener"\>average time per mask fell from 34 to 14 seconds\</a\> as the model improved. The authors report that 14 seconds is 6.5x faster than COCO mask annotation. By the end, 99.1% of the 1.1 billion released masks were generated fully automatically.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<h3\>Text: LLMs vs crowd workers\</h3\>&#xA;\<p\>A study in \<a href="https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2305016120" target="_blank" rel="noopener"\>PNAS\</a\> compared \<a href="https://openai.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener"\>OpenAI's\</a\> ChatGPT against crowd workers on \<a href="https://www.mturk.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener"\>Amazon Mechanical Turk\</a\> across 6,183 tweets and news articles. ChatGPT's zero-shot accuracy beat the crowd workers by about 25 percentage points on average. Each label cost under $0.003, about thirty times cheaper.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<h3\>Robotics: relabeling robot demonstrations with a vision-language model\</h3\>&#xA;\<p\>A \<a href="https://research.google/" target="_blank" rel="noopener"\>Google\</a\> robotics team had 80,000 robot demonstrations, but \<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11736" target="_blank" rel="noopener"\>96.5% of them had no crowd-sourced language labels\</a\>. Their method, DIAL, fine-tuned a vision-language model on the small labeled slice, then used it to write instructions for the rest. Policies trained on the augmented data generalized to 60 new instructions that weren't in the original set.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p class="pull"\>Look closely and every one of these wins starts with human labels. \<span\>Automation doesn't replace manual annotation. It spends it.\</span\>\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>SAM's first stage was human-led. The PNAS study measured accuracy against labels from trained research assistants. DIAL needed 2,800 human-annotated episodes before the model could label the other 77,000 or so. The quality of your automated labels is capped by the quality of the human seed you train and test them against.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>The market reflects how fast this shift is moving. \<a href="https://www.grandviewresearch.com/press-release/global-data-annotation-tools-market" target="_blank" rel="noopener"\>Grand View Research projects\</a\> the data annotation tools market will reach $5.33 billion by 2030, growing about 26% a year. From what we see in the market, the fastest-moving tools blend automation with human review rather than trying to remove people.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<h2 id="compare"\>Manual vs automated data annotation: head-to-head\</h2\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>Here's how the two approaches, plus the hybrid most teams end up with, compare on the factors that decide training outcomes.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<div class="table-scroll"\>&#xA;\<table\>&#xA; Once a model drafts the labels, your spend shifts from labeling to reviewing. And reviewing is a psychologically different job.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>A 2026 study in the \<a href="https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/nrcn4h7d" target="_blank" rel="noopener"\>Harvard Data Science Review\</a\> ran a randomized experiment with 2,784 people reviewing AI-suggested labels. Two findings stand out. When flagging an error required typing the corrected value, reviewers engaged less and accepted more wrong suggestions. And people who were more skeptical of AI were more accurate reviewers.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>In other words, the extra effort to reject a label pushes people toward approving it. Now ask what share of those 3,900 hours is real scrutiny versus scrolling. If reviewers spend two seconds glancing at a mask, you didn't buy review. You bought a timestamp.\</p\>&#xA;\</div\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>Good review design fixes most of this:\</p\>&#xA;\<ul\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Make rejection cheap.\</strong\> A reviewer should be able to flag a bad draft in one action and fix it later, or route it to someone else.\</li\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Seed honeypots.\</strong\> Slip known-wrong pre-labels into the queue. If reviewers approve them, your review isn't working.\</li\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Treat high acceptance as a question.\</strong\> A 97% approval rate means either the model is excellent or nobody is looking. You need a golden set to tell which.\</li\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Hide confidence scores sometimes.\</strong\> Showing "0.94" invites trust. Blind batches keep reviewers honest.\</li\>&#xA;\</ul\>&#xA;&#xA;\<h2 id="matrix"\>The Attention Budget Matrix: how to decide, label by label\</h2\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>Here's the framework we use. On video, a gripper closing around a part looks like a win. The force trace tells a different story. The part slipped, the operator recovered, and the "success" is really a recovery demonstration.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<dl class="sposo"\>&#xA;  \<dt\>Situation\</dt\>\<dd\>A team collects bimanual pick-and-place demonstrations and uses a vision-language model to tag each episode "success" or "fail" from the overhead camera.\</dd\>&#xA;  \<dt\>Problem\</dt\>\<dd\>Slips and regrasp recoveries get tagged as clean successes. The policy learns that a fumble-and-recover is the normal way to do the task.\</dd\>&#xA;  \<dt\>Solution\</dt\>\<dd\>Keep the model's tag as a suggestion. A trained reviewer confirms success using synchronized video and force data, and labels recoveries as their own category.\</dd\>&#xA;  \<dt\>Outcome\</dt\>\<dd\>Recoveries become useful training signal instead of noise, and the "clean success" set means what it says.\</dd\>&#xA;\</dl\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>This is also why we score every delivered episode on task success, trajectory smoothness, calibration drift, annotation consistency, and environment coverage before it reaches a client. It's the same idea as the matrix: automate the checks that are cheap to verify, and put people on the ones that aren't. More on that in our post on \<a href="https://www.gamasome.com/blog/physical-ai-data-quality/"\>physical AI data quality\</a\> and on \<a href="https://www.gamasome.com/blog/human-demonstrations-physical-ai/"\>human demonstrations for robot learning\</a\>.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>The research direction points the same way. A 2026 NeurIPS paper, DeMiAn, used an automatic VLM pipeline to \<a href="https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/poster/150612" target="_blank" rel="noopener"\>re-label over 1 million robot manipulation episodes and 50,000 egocentric human videos\</a\> with richer descriptions. With no new demonstrations collected, its learned instructor raised RoboCasa task success by 5 points. Automated annotation is becoming a real lever for robot learning. It works best on top of demonstrations that humans captured and verified well.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<h2 id="decide"\>Seven questions to ask before you choose\</h2\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>Run through these before committing budget to either approach. If you answer "no" or "not sure" to several, start more manual than you planned.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<ol class="check"\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Do we have a golden set?\</strong\> A few hundred to a few thousand items labeled carefully by people, used only for measuring. Without it, you can't tell whether automation is working.\</li\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Has a pre-labeling model seen this kind of data before?\</strong\> New sensors, new sites, and new object types push it out of its comfort zone.\</li\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>What does one wrong label cost downstream?\</strong\> A misranked search result is not a robot dropping a part.\</li\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Can a reviewer verify a label in seconds, with what's on screen?\</strong\> If they need extra context, it's a hard-check label.\</li\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Is our label set stable?\</strong\> If the taxonomy will change twice next quarter, programmatic or prompt-based labels are easier to regenerate than hand labels.\</li\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Can we slice errors by condition?\</strong\> Lighting, location, object type, operator. Systematic errors hide in averages.\</li\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Who owns review quality?\</strong\> Someone needs to track honeypot catch rates and acceptance rates, not just throughput.\</li\>&#xA;\</ol\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>Deciding \<em\>who\</em\> does this work is a separate call from \<em\>how\</em\> it's done. We cover that in \<a href="https://www.gamasome.com/blog/in-house-vs-outsourced-data-annotation/"\>in-house vs outsourced data annotation\</a\>.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<h2 id="perspective"\>Expert perspective: what we'd do if we started a dataset Monday\</h2\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>These are opinions, formed from running capture and annotation programs on real robots and real sites.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<ul\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Label the first batch by hand, in-house.\</strong\> Not to save money. To discover what your guidelines got wrong. The first few hundred items always break the taxonomy somewhere.\</li\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Build the golden set before you buy automation.\</strong\> Teams do it in the opposite order, then have no way to measure what they bought.\</li\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Choose automation per label layer, never per project.\</strong\> "We're an automated shop" is how high-cost, hard-to-check labels get waved through.\</li\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Hire skeptics as reviewers.\</strong\> The HDSR study backs up what we see: people who distrust the model catch more of its mistakes.\</li\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Watch acceptance rate like a smoke alarm.\</strong\> When it creeps toward 100%, run a honeypot batch that week.\</li\>&#xA;  \<li\>\<strong\>Budget for relabeling.\</strong\> Your label definitions will change. Programmatic labels, which \<a href="https://snorkel.ai/automated-data-labeling/" target="_blank" rel="noopener"\>Snorkel AI\</a\> and others champion, are cheap to regenerate. Hand labels aren't. Plan for both.\</li\>&#xA;\</ul\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>Where is this heading? Human annotators are moving from drawing labels to defining, auditing, and adjudicating them. Fewer people will draw polygons. More people will decide what a "successful grasp" means and prove that the machine agrees. That work is harder to automate, and it's where dataset quality is now decided.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<h2 id="faq"\>Frequently asked questions\</h2\>&#xA;&#xA;\<div class="faq"\>&#xA;  \<details open\>&#xA;    \<summary\>Is manual or automated data annotation better for AI training?\</summary\>&#xA;    \<p\>Neither wins across the board. Manual annotation is more reliable on ambiguous, novel, or high-stakes data. Automated annotation is faster and much cheaper at scale on well-defined tasks. Most production teams use a hybrid where models draft labels and humans verify the ones where a mistake would be costly or hard to spot.\</p\>&#xA;  \</details\>&#xA;  \<details\>&#xA;    \<summary\>Is manual annotation always more accurate than automated annotation?\</summary\>&#xA;    \<p\>No. A study of 10 widely used benchmark datasets estimated that about 3.3% of human-made test labels were wrong on average, including roughly 6% of the ImageNet validation set. Human labels are usually better on hard cases, but they still need consensus checks and audits.\</p\>&#xA;  \</details\>&#xA;  \<details\>&#xA;    \<summary\>How much cheaper is automated data annotation?\</summary\>&#xA;    \<p\>It depends on the task. A PNAS study found ChatGPT text labels cost under $0.003 each, about thirty times less than Amazon Mechanical Turk. Meta's Segment Anything data engine cut mask annotation time to 14 seconds, about 6.5 times faster than COCO-style manual masks. Review and audit time still has to be budgeted.\</p\>&#xA;  \</details\>&#xA;  \<details\>&#xA;    \<summary\>What is human-in-the-loop data annotation?\</summary\>&#xA;    \<p\>Human-in-the-loop annotation is a workflow where a model produces draft labels and people review, correct, or reject them. The corrections are fed back to improve the model. It works well when reviewers are trained to challenge the machine instead of approving its output by default.\</p\>&#xA;  \</details\>&#xA;  \<details\>&#xA;    \<summary\>When should you avoid automated data labeling?\</summary\>&#xA;    \<p\>Avoid relying on automated labels when a wrong label is costly and also expensive to verify, such as medical findings, safety-critical edge cases, new object classes the model has never seen, or task-success judgments in robot demonstrations that require watching the full episode with sensor data.\</p\>&#xA;  \</details\>&#xA;  \<details\>&#xA;    \<summary\>Can large language models and vision-language models replace human annotators?\</summary\>&#xA;    \<p\>They can replace a large share of routine labeling, especially text classification and captioning. They do not replace the humans who define the label set, build the golden reference data, and audit for systematic errors. Every major automated labeling success to date started from a human-labeled seed.\</p\>&#xA;  \</details\>&#xA;  \<details\>&#xA;    \<summary\>How do you check the quality of automated labels?\</summary\>&#xA;    \<p\>Compare them against a human-built golden set, slice error rates by data condition such as lighting or object type, seed known-wrong pre-labels to test whether reviewers catch them, and treat a very high review acceptance rate as a warning sign rather than a success metric.\</p\>&#xA;  \</details\>&#xA;  \<details\>&#xA;    \<summary\>Which annotation approach works best for robotics and physical AI data?\</summary\>&#xA;    \<p\>Robot episodes need a layered approach. Object masks, tracking, and calibration checks automate well. Language instructions can be drafted by a vision-language model and verified by people. Action boundaries, task success, and failure types should stay human-led because they depend on video, force data, and task intent together.\</p\>&#xA;  \</details\>&#xA;\</div\>&#xA;&#xA;\<h2 id="ending"\>Back to the shrink-wrapped pallets\</h2\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>Priya's team didn't go back to hand-labeling everything. That would have thrown away the large majority of masks the model got right. Instead, they sliced errors by condition, found the glare cluster, and moved wrapped pallets into the "pre-label, human signs off" quadrant with honeypots in the queue. Everything else stayed automated with sample audits.\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\<p\>The fix took a week. The lesson took longer to sink in: the model wasn't the weak point. The review design was. So when someone asks whether manual or automated annotation is better, the honest answer is a second question. Where are you putting your people, and how do you know they're really looking?\</p\>&#xA;&#xA;\</div\>&#xA;\</article\>&#xA;&#xA;\<section class="final-cta"\>&#xA;  \<div class="wrap"\>&#xA;    \<div class="eyebrow" style="justify-content\:center;"\>Next step\</div\>&#xA;    \<h2\>Need a labeling plan that holds up in deployment?\</h2\>&#xA;    \<p\>Tell us your data type, label set, and model.
