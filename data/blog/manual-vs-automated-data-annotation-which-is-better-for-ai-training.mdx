\# Manual vs Automated Data Annotation: Which Is Better for AI Training?

When Meta built the dataset behind its Segment Anything model, people drew the first 4.3 million masks with a model helping them. By the end of the project, \[99.1 percent of the 1.1 billion released masks had been generated fully automatically\](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02643). It is the most cited proof that automated data annotation works at a scale no human team could ever match.

Read the paper closely, though, and it proves something else too. The automation only worked because humans went first. They labeled the early batches, corrected the model's drafts, and stayed in the loop until the machine was good enough to trust. The 99.1 percent sits on top of the human part, not instead of it.

That is the decision every AI team faces before training: label the data by hand, let a model label it, or split the work. Most guides answer "use a hybrid" and stop. This one goes further. It shows where the human should actually sit, and why getting that wrong costs more than either extreme. If you need the basics first, start with our explainer on \[what data annotation is\](/blog/what-is-data-annotation/).

\## The short answer

Neither approach is better across the board. \*\*Manual annotation\*\* is more reliable on ambiguous, novel, or high-stakes data. \*\*Automated annotation\*\* is faster and far cheaper at scale on well-defined tasks. Most production teams now run a hybrid: models draft the labels, and people verify the ones where a mistake would be costly or hard to catch.

Four takeaways to keep in mind as you read:

\- Automation doesn't remove human labeling. It multiplies a small, carefully human-labeled seed.
\- Manual errors are scattered. Automated errors repeat. Models learn repeated errors very well.
\- The real cost of automation is review, and badly designed review turns people into rubber stamps.
\- Decide label by label, not project by project.

\## A 97 percent acceptance rate that should have been a warning

\*The scenario below is a composite built from patterns we see across annotation programs, not a single named customer.\*

Priya leads perception at a warehouse robotics startup. Her team needed 40,000 segmentation masks of pallets, totes, and cartons before a pilot with a logistics customer. Hand labeling would take two months. A foundation segmentation model could pre-label everything over a weekend.

They chose the model. Reviewers cleared the queue in nine days, and the acceptance rate came in at 97 percent. The team celebrated.

Three weeks into the pilot, the robot started misjudging grasp points on shrink-wrapped pallets. Glare off the plastic wrap had fooled the pre-labeler into drawing masks a few centimeters too wide, on almost every wrapped pallet. The reviewers, moving fast through thousands of almost-right outlines, had approved them.

Here's the uncomfortable part. Hand labeling would have produced errors too. But tired people make different mistakes on different images. This model made the same mistake on the same kind of image thousands of times, and the robot learned it perfectly.

That story is why "manual or automated?" is the wrong question. The useful question is this: \*\*where does human attention go, and what happens to the labels nobody really looks at?\*\*

\## Manual vs automated is a spectrum, not a switch

Most comparisons frame this as a binary choice. In practice, teams use four common setups. They differ in who draws the label and who checks it.

1\. \*\*Manual from scratch.\*\* A trained person draws every label, and a second annotator or QA sample checks the work. This fits new classes, ambiguous data, and medical or safety-critical cases.
2\. \*\*Model-assisted pre-labeling.\*\* A model drafts each label and a person corrects it, item by item. This is the common setup for segmentation, bounding boxes, and object tracking.
3\. \*\*Programmatic labeling, also called weak supervision.\*\* Domain experts write rules that label data in bulk, and the results are checked statistically against a reference set. This works well for large text pools and document classification.
4\. \*\*Fully automated labeling.\*\* A foundation model or large language model labels everything, and people audit a sample. This suits common object classes, captions, and relabeling old archives.

Notice that every option still has a person in it somewhere. You are never choosing between humans and machines. You are choosing where the human sits and how much attention each label gets. If the line between annotation and labeling feels fuzzy, our breakdown of \[data annotation vs data labeling\](/blog/data-annotation-vs-data-labeling/) clears it up.

\## Where hand labeling still earns its cost

The usual case for manual annotation is simple: humans are more accurate. That's only half true, and the false half matters.

Researchers from MIT and Amazon audited the test sets of 10 of the most widely used computer vision, language, and audio benchmarks. They \[estimated an average label error rate of 3.3 percent\](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14749), including 2,916 wrong labels in the ImageNet validation set, about 6 percent of it. These were carefully built, human-labeled datasets used across the entire field. You can \[browse the mislabeled examples yourself\](https://labelerrors.com/).

The same study found something sharper. On ImageNet with corrected labels, the smaller ResNet-18 beat ResNet-50 once the share of originally mislabeled test examples rose by just 6 percent. In plain terms, a few points of bad labels can flip which model you believe is best.

So manual annotation isn't valuable because people are flawless. It's valuable because people are good at three things models still struggle with:

\- \*\*Noticing that the label set itself is wrong.\*\* A person sees that "damaged carton" really needs two sub-classes. A model just picks one.
\- \*\*Handling the first examples of anything new.\*\* A new product, a new tool, a new failure mode. A model can't pre-label what it has never seen.
\- \*\*Judging intent.\*\* Did the robot succeed, or did it get lucky? Is that product review sarcastic? That's judgment, not pattern matching.

Manual annotation is also slow, and it barely gets cheaper as you scale. The ten-thousandth label costs about what the first one did. That's the price of judgment.

\## Three places automation has already won

Automation crossed a line in the last few years. On some tasks, it isn't just faster than people. It beats the cheapest human option outright.

\### Images: Meta's Segment Anything data engine

\[Meta\](https://ai.meta.com/) built its SA-1B dataset with a three-stage data engine. In the human-led first stage, \[average annotation time per mask fell from 34 seconds to 14\](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02643) as the model improved. The authors report that 14 seconds is 6.5 times faster than COCO-style manual mask annotation. By the final stage, the model was labeling on its own.

\### Text: large language models vs crowd workers

A study published in \[PNAS\](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2305016120) compared \[OpenAI's\](https://openai.com/) ChatGPT with crowd workers on \[Amazon Mechanical Turk\](https://www.mturk.com/) across 6,183 tweets and news articles. ChatGPT's zero-shot accuracy beat the crowd workers by about 25 percentage points on average. Each label cost under $0.003, roughly thirty times cheaper.

\### Robots: relabeling demonstrations with a vision-language model

A \[Google\](https://research.google/) robotics team had 80,000 robot demonstrations, but \[96.5 percent of them had no crowd-sourced language labels\](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11736). Their method, called DIAL, fine-tuned a vision-language model on the small labeled slice and used it to write instructions for the rest. Robot policies trained on the result handled 60 new instructions that never appeared in the original data.

\### What all three have in common

Look closely and every one of these wins starts with human labels. SAM's first stage was human-led. The PNAS study scored ChatGPT against labels from trained research assistants. DIAL needed 2,800 human-annotated episodes before the model could label the other 77,000 or so.

\> \*\*Automation doesn't replace manual annotation. It spends it.\*\* The quality ceiling of your automated labels is set by the human-labeled seed you train and test them against. Skimp on the seed and you are scaling a guess.

The market is moving the same way. \[Grand View Research projects\](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/press-release/global-data-annotation-tools-market) the data annotation tools market will reach $5.33 billion by 2030, growing about 26 percent a year. From what we see, the fastest-moving tools blend automation with human review rather than trying to remove people.

\## How manual and automated annotation compare

Here's how the two approaches, plus the hybrid most teams land on, stack up on the factors that decide training outcomes.

\*\*Accuracy on clear-cut data.\*\* All three do well here. Manual work carries a little fatigue noise. Automated labels are strong once the model is tuned.

\*\*Accuracy on ambiguous or new data.\*\* Manual wins, especially with expert annotators. Automated labeling is weak here and fails silently. A hybrid performs well only if reviewers are trained to push back on the model.

\*\*Speed.\*\* Manual is slow and grows with headcount. Automated is the fastest by a wide margin. A hybrid is fast but capped by how quickly people can review.

\*\*Cost as volume grows.\*\* Manual cost stays flat per label. Automated cost falls sharply after setup. Hybrid cost falls too, but review cost never disappears.

\*\*Consistency.\*\* Manual labels drift between people and between shifts. Automated labels are perfectly consistent, including their mistakes. A hybrid stays consistent with human correction on top.

\*\*Typical error pattern.\*\* Manual errors are random and scattered. Automated errors are systematic and repeated. Hybrid errors come from reviewers approving bad drafts.

\*\*Handling guideline changes.\*\* Manual teams need retraining, which is slow but flexible. Automated pipelines just re-run their rules or prompts. A hybrid adapts quickly with spot re-review.

\*\*Best fit.\*\* Manual suits golden reference sets, edge cases, and high-stakes labels. Automated suits high-volume, low-risk classes. A hybrid suits most production datasets.

Most guides skip consistency and error pattern. They're the two factors that caused Priya's problem, so they're worth a closer look.

\## Automation changes the shape of your errors

Here's our contrarian take. Teams often switch to automation to "reduce human error." It usually does reduce the \*number\* of errors. But it changes their \*shape\*, and shape matters more than count.

Random errors from people tend to wash out in training, as long as there aren't too many. The model sees the right label far more often than any one wrong version. Systematic errors don't wash out. They appear every time the same condition shows up, whether that's glare, a rare object, a camera angle, or a regional dialect. The model treats them as a real pattern and learns them.

Each approach leaves its own error signature, and each one needs a different check:

\- \*\*Scattered noise comes from manual work.\*\* Its sources are fatigue, unclear guidelines, and drift between annotators. It lowers your accuracy ceiling but mostly averages out. Catch it with overlap labeling and agreement scores.
\- \*\*Systematic bias comes from automation.\*\* It traces back to a pre-labeler's blind spot tied to a specific data condition. It hurts because the model learns the error as a rule. Catch it by slicing error rates by lighting, class, site, and sensor.
\- \*\*Rubber-stamping comes from poorly run hybrids.\*\* Reviewers anchor on the draft and approve by default. It hurts because bad labels now carry a "verified" flag. Catch it by seeding known-wrong drafts and measuring how many reviewers catch.

A 2021 Google Research study, titled "\[Everyone wants to do the model work, not the data work\](https://storage.googleapis.com/gweb-research2023-media/pubtools/5936.pdf)," found that 92 percent of the AI practitioners it interviewed had experienced at least one "data cascade." That's a data problem that surfaces as a failure much later in the pipeline. Nearly half, 45.3 percent, had hit two or more in a single project. A systematic labeling error is a textbook cascade. It looks fine in review and fails in deployment.

\## The cost nobody budgets for: review

Once a model drafts the labels, your spending moves from labeling to reviewing. Most cost comparisons stop there and call it savings. But reviewing is a different job psychologically, and it fails in a predictable way.

A 2026 study in the \[Harvard Data Science Review\](https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/nrcn4h7d) ran a randomized experiment with 2,784 people checking AI-suggested labels. When flagging an error meant typing in the corrected value, reviewers engaged less and accepted more wrong suggestions. People who were more skeptical of AI turned out to be more accurate reviewers.

Put simply, extra effort to reject a label pushes people toward approving it. If your tool makes "accept" one click and "fix" ten clicks, you've built a rubber-stamp machine.

Here's a quick way to see what's at stake. Take the Segment Anything paper's numbers for one million masks. COCO-style manual masks at about 91 seconds each add up to roughly 25,300 annotator hours. Model-assisted masks at 14 seconds each add up to roughly 3,900 hours. That's about 21,000 hours saved. Now ask how much of the remaining 3,900 hours is real scrutiny and how much is scrolling. If a reviewer glances at each mask for two seconds, you didn't buy review. You bought a timestamp.

Good review design fixes most of this:

\- \*\*Make rejection cheap.\*\* Reviewers should be able to flag a bad draft in one action and fix it later or pass it on.
\- \*\*Seed honeypots.\*\* Slip known-wrong pre-labels into the queue. If reviewers approve them, your review isn't working.
\- \*\*Treat high acceptance as a question.\*\* A 97 percent approval rate means either the model is excellent or nobody is looking. Only a golden set can tell you which.
\- \*\*Hide confidence scores sometimes.\*\* Showing "0.94" next to a label invites trust. Blind batches keep reviewers honest.

\## The Attention Budget Matrix: decide label by label

Here's the framework we use with teams. Instead of choosing manual or automated for a whole project, score each type of label on two questions:

1\. \*\*How costly is a wrong label downstream?\*\* Think about what actually happens: a missed tumor margin, a robot dropping a part, a mistagged product.
2\. \*\*How cheaply can a person verify the machine's label?\*\* Can they confirm it in one glance, or do they need to watch a full video with sensor data?

Those two answers put every label type into one of four zones.

\### Low cost of error, cheap to check: automate and sample

Think bounding boxes on common items in a product catalog. A wrong box rarely breaks anything, and anyone can spot one instantly. Run random audits on each batch. When you find a pattern, fix the model or the prompt, not individual labels.

\### High cost of error, cheap to check: pre-label, then a human signs off

Think pallet and tote masks that drive a robot's grasp planning. Errors are expensive, but a trained eye catches them fast. This is where Priya's labels belonged. Every item gets real review, with honeypots in the queue to prove the review is real.

\### Low cost of error, hard to check: automate and measure statistically

Think topic tags on millions of support tickets. Checking each one costs more than the occasional wrong tag. Track accuracy against a golden set, broken out by category, and accept a known, bounded noise rate.

\### High cost of error, hard to check: human-led and double-annotated

Think lesion boundaries in medical scans, or whether a robot actually completed a task. Experts label from scratch, two people per item, and disagreements go to adjudication. Models can suggest. They don't decide.

The trap is a label from that last zone that looks like it belongs in the second. It seems easy to check on screen, so the team automates it. But confirming it really needs context the reviewer doesn't have. That's where the most expensive failures hide, and robot data is full of them.

\## Robot data plays by different rules

Most manual vs automated comparisons assume a static image and a box around an object. Robot training data isn't that. A single teleoperated episode can include four camera streams, joint positions, gripper state, and a force-torque trace across a minute of continuous motion. Our guide to \[what physical AI is\](/blog/what-is-physical-ai/) covers why this data behaves so differently.

That means one episode carries several layers of labels, and each layer lands in a different zone of the matrix:

\- \*\*Camera calibration and sync checks: fully automated.\*\* It's math, not judgment, and thresholds catch drift reliably.
\- \*\*Object masks and tracking: model-assisted with human sign-off.\*\* Foundation segmentation models handle this well, and errors are visible at a glance.
\- \*\*Trajectory quality: automated metrics, with people reviewing flagged episodes.\*\* Jerky motion and oscillation are measurable. Whether a correction was intentional isn't.
\- \*\*Language instructions: drafted by a vision-language model, verified by a person.\*\* DIAL shows this scales. But a wrong instruction still teaches the wrong behavior.
\- \*\*Action boundaries, like when a grasp starts or a release ends: human-led, model-suggested.\*\* These depend on physical contact, which lives in the force data, not the video.
\- \*\*Task success and failure type: human, ideally double-labeled.\*\* On camera, a clean success and a near-miss can look identical.

\### What we see in the field

Across our \[physical AI data collection\](/services/physical-ai-data-collection/) programs, the label teams automate first is often the one that should be automated last: whether the episode succeeded. On video, a gripper closing around a part looks like a win. The force trace tells a different story. The part slipped, the operator recovered, and the "success" is really a recovery demonstration.

Here's how that plays out:

\- \*\*Situation:\*\* A team collects two-armed pick-and-place demonstrations and has a vision-language model tag each episode "success" or "fail" from the overhead camera.
\- \*\*Problem:\*\* Slips and regrasp recoveries get tagged as clean successes. The policy learns that fumbling and recovering is the normal way to do the task.
\- \*\*Solution:\*\* The model's tag becomes a suggestion. A trained reviewer confirms success using synchronized video and force data, and recoveries get their own category.
\- \*\*Outcome:\*\* Recoveries become useful training signal instead of noise, and the "clean success" set finally means what it says.

This is the same reasoning behind how we score delivered episodes on task success, trajectory smoothness, calibration drift, annotation consistency, and environment coverage before they reach a client. Automate the checks that are cheap to verify, and put people on the ones that aren't. We go deeper in \[physical AI data quality\](/blog/physical-ai-data-quality/) and our guide to \[human demonstrations for robot learning\](/blog/human-demonstrations-physical-ai/).

Research is heading the same way. A 2026 NeurIPS paper, DeMiAn, used an automatic vision-language pipeline to \[re-label more than 1 million robot manipulation episodes and 50,000 egocentric human videos\](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/poster/150612) with richer descriptions. Without collecting a single new demonstration, its learned instructor raised RoboCasa task success by 5 points. Automated annotation is becoming a real lever for robot learning, as long as it sits on top of demonstrations people captured and verified well.

\## Mistakes teams make before they label a single item

\*\*Buying automation before building a golden set.\*\* Without a few hundred to a few thousand items labeled carefully by people and used only for measurement, there's no way to tell whether automated labels are any good. Teams that buy the tool first end up trusting a number they can't check.

\*\*Picking one method for the whole project.\*\* "We're an automated shop" is how high-cost, hard-to-check labels get waved through alongside the easy ones. The choice belongs at the level of each label type.

\*\*Reading acceptance rate as quality.\*\* A review queue that approves nearly everything looks like success on a dashboard. It's just as likely to mean nobody is really looking. Honeypot catch rate tells you which.

\*\*Averaging away the errors that matter.\*\* A strong overall accuracy score can hide poor accuracy on the one condition your deployment site is full of. Slice error rates by lighting, location, object type, and operator, or systematic errors stay invisible.

\*\*A note on honesty.\*\* Read the automation headlines carefully. The PNAS study showed ChatGPT beating crowd workers, not trained experts, whose labels were the yardstick. SAM's automatic masks were judged against human-made ones. Even "gold standard" benchmarks carry a few percent of human error. Nobody in this debate has a perfect labeler, and any vendor implying otherwise, human or machine, deserves a closer look.

\## Seven questions to ask before you choose

Run through these before you commit budget. If you answer "no" or "not sure" to several, start more manual than you planned.

1\. \*\*Do we have a golden set?\*\* Without one, you can't measure whether automation is working.
2\. \*\*Has a pre-labeling model seen this kind of data before?\*\* New sensors, sites, and object types push models out of their comfort zone.
3\. \*\*What does one wrong label cost downstream?\*\* A misranked search result is not a robot dropping a part.
4\. \*\*Can a reviewer verify a label in seconds with what's on screen?\*\* If they need extra context, it's a hard-to-check label.
5\. \*\*Is our label set stable?\*\* If the categories will change twice next quarter, programmatic or prompt-based labels are easier to regenerate than hand labels.
6\. \*\*Can we slice errors by condition?\*\* Systematic errors hide inside averages.
7\. \*\*Who owns review quality?\*\* Someone needs to track honeypot catch rates and acceptance rates, not just throughput.

\## If you were starting a dataset on Monday

Label the first batch by hand, in-house, even if you plan to automate later. The goal isn't saving money. It's finding out what your guidelines got wrong, because the first few hundred items almost always break the taxonomy somewhere. Turn the best of that batch into your golden set before you buy or build any automation.

Then score every label type on the Attention Budget Matrix and decide its workflow on its own terms. Staff the review queue with people who are a little suspicious of the model, give them a one-click reject, and run a honeypot batch whenever the acceptance rate creeps toward 100 percent. Budget for relabeling too. Your label definitions will change, and programmatic labels, an approach \[Snorkel AI\](https://snorkel.ai/automated-data-labeling/) and others champion, are far cheaper to regenerate than hand labels.

Who does this work is a separate decision from how it gets done. We cover that side in \[in-house vs outsourced data annotation\](/blog/in-house-vs-outsourced-data-annotation/). The bigger shift is already underway: annotators are moving from drawing labels to defining, auditing, and adjudicating them. Fewer people will trace polygons. More will decide what a "successful grasp" means and prove the machine agrees.

\## Frequently asked questions

\### Is manual or automated data annotation better for AI training?

Neither wins across the board. Manual annotation is more reliable on ambiguous, novel, or high-stakes data. Automated annotation is faster and much cheaper at scale on well-defined tasks. Most production teams use a hybrid where models draft labels and humans verify the ones where a mistake would be costly or hard to spot.

\### Is manual annotation always more accurate than automated annotation?

No. A study of 10 widely used benchmark datasets estimated that about 3.3 percent of human-made test labels were wrong on average, including roughly 6 percent of the ImageNet validation set. Human labels are usually better on hard cases, but they still need consensus checks and audits.

\### How much cheaper is automated data annotation?

It depends on the task. A PNAS study found ChatGPT text labels cost under $0.003 each, about thirty times less than Amazon Mechanical Turk. Meta's Segment Anything data engine cut mask annotation time to 14 seconds, about 6.5 times faster than COCO-style manual masks. Review and audit time still has to be budgeted.

\### What is human-in-the-loop data annotation?

Human-in-the-loop annotation is a workflow where a model produces draft labels and people review, correct, or reject them. The corrections are fed back to improve the model. It works well when reviewers are trained to challenge the machine instead of approving its output by default.

\### When should you avoid automated data labeling?

Avoid relying on automated labels when a wrong label is costly and also expensive to verify. That includes medical findings, safety-critical edge cases, new object classes the model has never seen, and task-success judgments in robot demonstrations that require watching the full episode with sensor data.

\### Can large language models and vision-language models replace human annotators?

They can replace a large share of routine labeling, especially text classification and captioning. They don't replace the people who define the label set, build the golden reference data, and audit for systematic errors. Every major automated labeling success so far started from a human-labeled seed.

\### How do you check the quality of automated labels?

Compare them against a human-built golden set, break error rates out by data condition such as lighting or object type, seed known-wrong pre-labels to test whether reviewers catch them, and treat a very high review acceptance rate as a warning sign rather than a success metric.

\### Which annotation approach works best for robotics and physical AI data?

Robot episodes need a layered approach. Object masks, tracking, and calibration checks automate well. Language instructions can be drafted by a vision-language model and verified by people. Action boundaries, task success, and failure types should stay human-led because they depend on video, force data, and task intent together.

\## Back to the shrink-wrapped pallets

Priya's team didn't go back to hand labeling everything. That would have thrown away the large majority of masks the model got right. Instead, they sliced their errors by condition, found the glare cluster, and moved wrapped pallets into the "pre-label, then a human signs off" zone with honeypots in the queue. Everything else stayed automated with sample audits.

The fix took a week. The lesson took longer to sink in: the model was never the weak point. The review design was.

So when someone asks whether manual or automated annotation is better, the honest answer is another question. Where are your people, and how do you know they're really looking? In plain terms, a few points of bad labels can flip which model you believe is best.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;So manual annotation isn't valuable because people are flawless. It's valuable because people are good at three things models still struggle with:&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;- **Noticing that the label set itself is wrong.** A person sees that "damaged carton" really needs two sub-classes. A model just picks one.&#xD;&#xA;- **Handling the first examples of anything new.** A new product, a new tool, a new failure mode. A model can't pre-label what it has never seen.&#xD;&#xA;- **Judging intent.** Did the robot succeed, or did it get lucky? Is that product review sarcastic? That's judgment, not pattern matching.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Manual annotation is also slow, and it barely gets cheaper as you scale. The ten-thousandth label costs about what the first one did. That's the price of judgment.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## Three places automation has already won&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Automation crossed a line in the last few years. On some tasks, it isn't just faster than people. It beats the cheapest human option outright.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### Images: Meta's Segment Anything data engine&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;\[Meta](https://ai.meta.com/) built its SA-1B dataset with a three-stage data engine. In the human-led first stage, \[average annotation time per mask fell from 34 seconds to 14](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02643) as the model improved. The authors report that 14 seconds is 6.5 times faster than COCO-style manual mask annotation. By the final stage, the model was labeling on its own.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### Text: large language models vs crowd workers&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;A study published in \[PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2305016120) compared \[OpenAI's](https://openai.com/) ChatGPT with crowd workers on \[Amazon Mechanical Turk](https://www.mturk.com/) across 6,183 tweets and news articles. ChatGPT's zero-shot accuracy beat the crowd workers by about 25 percentage points on average. Each label cost under $0.003, roughly thirty times cheaper.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### Robots: relabeling demonstrations with a vision-language model&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;A \[Google](https://research.google/) robotics team had 80,000 robot demonstrations, but \[96.5 percent of them had no crowd-sourced language labels](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11736). Their method, called DIAL, fine-tuned a vision-language model on the small labeled slice and used it to write instructions for the rest. Robot policies trained on the result handled 60 new instructions that never appeared in the original data.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### What all three have in common&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Look closely and every one of these wins starts with human labels. SAM's first stage was human-led. The PNAS study scored ChatGPT against labels from trained research assistants. DIAL needed 2,800 human-annotated episodes before the model could label the other 77,000 or so.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;\> **Automation doesn't replace manual annotation. It spends it.** The quality ceiling of your automated labels is set by the human-labeled seed you train and test them against. Skimp on the seed and you are scaling a guess.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;The market is moving the same way. \[Grand View Research projects](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/press-release/global-data-annotation-tools-market) the data annotation tools market will reach $5.33 billion by 2030, growing about 26 percent a year. From what we see, the fastest-moving tools blend automation with human review rather than trying to remove people.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## How manual and automated annotation compare&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Here's how the two approaches, plus the hybrid most teams land on, stack up on the factors that decide training outcomes.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;**Accuracy on clear-cut data.** All three do well here. Manual work carries a little fatigue noise. Automated labels are strong once the model is tuned.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;**Accuracy on ambiguous or new data.** Manual wins, especially with expert annotators. Automated labeling is weak here and fails silently. A hybrid performs well only if reviewers are trained to push back on the model.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;**Speed.** Manual is slow and grows with headcount. Automated is the fastest by a wide margin. A hybrid is fast but capped by how quickly people can review.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;**Cost as volume grows.** Manual cost stays flat per label. Automated cost falls sharply after setup. Hybrid cost falls too, but review cost never disappears.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;**Consistency.** Manual labels drift between people and between shifts. Automated labels are perfectly consistent, including their mistakes. A hybrid stays consistent with human correction on top.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;**Typical error pattern.** Manual errors are random and scattered. Automated errors are systematic and repeated. Hybrid errors come from reviewers approving bad drafts.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;**Handling guideline changes.** Manual teams need retraining, which is slow but flexible. Automated pipelines just re-run their rules or prompts. A hybrid adapts quickly with spot re-review.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;**Best fit.** Manual suits golden reference sets, edge cases, and high-stakes labels. Automated suits high-volume, low-risk classes. A hybrid suits most production datasets.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Most guides skip consistency and error pattern. They're the two factors that caused Priya's problem, so they're worth a closer look.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## Automation changes the shape of your errors&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Here's our contrarian take. Teams often switch to automation to "reduce human error." It usually does reduce the *number* of errors. But it changes their *shape*, and shape matters more than count.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Random errors from people tend to wash out in training, as long as there aren't too many. The model sees the right label far more often than any one wrong version. Systematic errors don't wash out. They appear every time the same condition shows up, whether that's glare, a rare object, a camera angle, or a regional dialect. The model treats them as a real pattern and learns them.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Each approach leaves its own error signature, and each one needs a different check:&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;- **Scattered noise comes from manual work.** Its sources are fatigue, unclear guidelines, and drift between annotators. It lowers your accuracy ceiling but mostly averages out. Catch it with overlap labeling and agreement scores.&#xD;&#xA;- **Systematic bias comes from automation.** It traces back to a pre-labeler's blind spot tied to a specific data condition. It hurts because the model learns the error as a rule. Catch it by slicing error rates by lighting, class, site, and sensor.&#xD;&#xA;- **Rubber-stamping comes from poorly run hybrids.** Reviewers anchor on the draft and approve by default. It hurts because bad labels now carry a "verified" flag. Catch it by seeding known-wrong drafts and measuring how many reviewers catch.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;A 2021 Google Research study, titled "\[Everyone wants to do the model work, not the data work](https://storage.googleapis.com/gweb-research2023-media/pubtools/5936.pdf)," found that 92 percent of the AI practitioners it interviewed had experienced at least one "data cascade." That's a data problem that surfaces as a failure much later in the pipeline. Nearly half, 45.3 percent, had hit two or more in a single project. A systematic labeling error is a textbook cascade. It looks fine in review and fails in deployment.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## The cost nobody budgets for: review&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Once a model drafts the labels, your spending moves from labeling to reviewing. Most cost comparisons stop there and call it savings. But reviewing is a different job psychologically, and it fails in a predictable way.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;A 2026 study in the \[Harvard Data Science Review](https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/nrcn4h7d) ran a randomized experiment with 2,784 people checking AI-suggested labels. When flagging an error meant typing in the corrected value, reviewers engaged less and accepted more wrong suggestions. People who were more skeptical of AI turned out to be more accurate reviewers.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Put simply, extra effort to reject a label pushes people toward approving it. If your tool makes "accept" one click and "fix" ten clicks, you've built a rubber-stamp machine.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Here's a quick way to see what's at stake. Take the Segment Anything paper's numbers for one million masks. COCO-style manual masks at about 91 seconds each add up to roughly 25,300 annotator hours. Model-assisted masks at 14 seconds each add up to roughly 3,900 hours. That's about 21,000 hours saved. Now ask how much of the remaining 3,900 hours is real scrutiny and how much is scrolling. If a reviewer glances at each mask for two seconds, you didn't buy review. You bought a timestamp.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Good review design fixes most of this:&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;- **Make rejection cheap.** Reviewers should be able to flag a bad draft in one action and fix it later or pass it on.&#xD;&#xA;- **Seed honeypots.** Slip known-wrong pre-labels into the queue. If reviewers approve them, your review isn't working.&#xD;&#xA;- **Treat high acceptance as a question.** A 97 percent approval rate means either the model is excellent or nobody is looking. Only a golden set can tell you which.&#xD;&#xA;- **Hide confidence scores sometimes.** Showing "0.94" next to a label invites trust. Blind batches keep reviewers honest.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## The Attention Budget Matrix: decide label by label&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Here's the framework we use with teams. Instead of choosing manual or automated for a whole project, score each type of label on two questions:&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;1\. **How costly is a wrong label downstream?** Think about what actually happens: a missed tumor margin, a robot dropping a part, a mistagged product.&#xD;&#xA;2\. **How cheaply can a person verify the machine's label?** Can they confirm it in one glance, or do they need to watch a full video with sensor data?&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Those two answers put every label type into one of four zones.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### Low cost of error, cheap to check: automate and sample&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Think bounding boxes on common items in a product catalog. A wrong box rarely breaks anything, and anyone can spot one instantly. Run random audits on each batch. When you find a pattern, fix the model or the prompt, not individual labels.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### High cost of error, cheap to check: pre-label, then a human signs off&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Think pallet and tote masks that drive a robot's grasp planning. Errors are expensive, but a trained eye catches them fast. This is where Priya's labels belonged. Every item gets real review, with honeypots in the queue to prove the review is real.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### Low cost of error, hard to check: automate and measure statistically&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Think topic tags on millions of support tickets. Checking each one costs more than the occasional wrong tag. Track accuracy against a golden set, broken out by category, and accept a known, bounded noise rate.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### High cost of error, hard to check: human-led and double-annotated&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Think lesion boundaries in medical scans, or whether a robot actually completed a task. Experts label from scratch, two people per item, and disagreements go to adjudication. Models can suggest. They don't decide.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;The trap is a label from that last zone that looks like it belongs in the second. It seems easy to check on screen, so the team automates it. But confirming it really needs context the reviewer doesn't have. That's where the most expensive failures hide, and robot data is full of them.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## Robot data plays by different rules&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Most manual vs automated comparisons assume a static image and a box around an object. Robot training data isn't that. A single teleoperated episode can include four camera streams, joint positions, gripper state, and a force-torque trace across a minute of continuous motion. Our guide to \[what physical AI is](/blog/what-is-physical-ai/) covers why this data behaves so differently.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;That means one episode carries several layers of labels, and each layer lands in a different zone of the matrix:&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;- **Camera calibration and sync checks: fully automated.** It's math, not judgment, and thresholds catch drift reliably.&#xD;&#xA;- **Object masks and tracking: model-assisted with human sign-off.** Foundation segmentation models handle this well, and errors are visible at a glance.&#xD;&#xA;- **Trajectory quality: automated metrics, with people reviewing flagged episodes.** Jerky motion and oscillation are measurable. Whether a correction was intentional isn't.&#xD;&#xA;- **Language instructions: drafted by a vision-language model, verified by a person.** DIAL shows this scales. But a wrong instruction still teaches the wrong behavior.&#xD;&#xA;- **Action boundaries, like when a grasp starts or a release ends: human-led, model-suggested.** These depend on physical contact, which lives in the force data, not the video.&#xD;&#xA;- **Task success and failure type: human, ideally double-labeled.** On camera, a clean success and a near-miss can look identical.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### What we see in the field&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Across our \[physical AI data collection](/services/physical-ai-data-collection/) programs, the label teams automate first is often the one that should be automated last: whether the episode succeeded. On video, a gripper closing around a part looks like a win. The force trace tells a different story. The part slipped, the operator recovered, and the "success" is really a recovery demonstration.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Here's how that plays out:&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;- **Situation:** A team collects two-armed pick-and-place demonstrations and has a vision-language model tag each episode "success" or "fail" from the overhead camera.&#xD;&#xA;- **Problem:** Slips and regrasp recoveries get tagged as clean successes. The policy learns that fumbling and recovering is the normal way to do the task.&#xD;&#xA;- **Solution:** The model's tag becomes a suggestion. A trained reviewer confirms success using synchronized video and force data, and recoveries get their own category.&#xD;&#xA;- **Outcome:** Recoveries become useful training signal instead of noise, and the "clean success" set finally means what it says.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;This is the same reasoning behind how we score delivered episodes on task success, trajectory smoothness, calibration drift, annotation consistency, and environment coverage before they reach a client. Automate the checks that are cheap to verify, and put people on the ones that aren't. We go deeper in \[physical AI data quality](/blog/physical-ai-data-quality/) and our guide to \[human demonstrations for robot learning](/blog/human-demonstrations-physical-ai/).&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Research is heading the same way. A 2026 NeurIPS paper, DeMiAn, used an automatic vision-language pipeline to \[re-label more than 1 million robot manipulation episodes and 50,000 egocentric human videos](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/poster/150612) with richer descriptions. Without collecting a single new demonstration, its learned instructor raised RoboCasa task success by 5 points. Automated annotation is becoming a real lever for robot learning, as long as it sits on top of demonstrations people captured and verified well.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## Mistakes teams make before they label a single item&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;**Buying automation before building a golden set.** Without a few hundred to a few thousand items labeled carefully by people and used only for measurement, there's no way to tell whether automated labels are any good. Teams that buy the tool first end up trusting a number they can't check.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;**Picking one method for the whole project.** "We're an automated shop" is how high-cost, hard-to-check labels get waved through alongside the easy ones. The choice belongs at the level of each label type.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;**Reading acceptance rate as quality.** A review queue that approves nearly everything looks like success on a dashboard. It's just as likely to mean nobody is really looking. Honeypot catch rate tells you which.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;**Averaging away the errors that matter.** A strong overall accuracy score can hide poor accuracy on the one condition your deployment site is full of. Slice error rates by lighting, location, object type, and operator, or systematic errors stay invisible.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;**A note on honesty.** Read the automation headlines carefully. The PNAS study showed ChatGPT beating crowd workers, not trained experts, whose labels were the yardstick. SAM's automatic masks were judged against human-made ones. Even "gold standard" benchmarks carry a few percent of human error. Nobody in this debate has a perfect labeler, and any vendor implying otherwise, human or machine, deserves a closer look.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## Seven questions to ask before you choose&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Run through these before you commit budget. If you answer "no" or "not sure" to several, start more manual than you planned.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;1\. **Do we have a golden set?** Without one, you can't measure whether automation is working.&#xD;&#xA;2\. **Has a pre-labeling model seen this kind of data before?** New sensors, sites, and object types push models out of their comfort zone.&#xD;&#xA;3\. **What does one wrong label cost downstream?** A misranked search result is not a robot dropping a part.&#xD;&#xA;4\. **Can a reviewer verify a label in seconds with what's on screen?** If they need extra context, it's a hard-to-check label.&#xD;&#xA;5\. **Is our label set stable?** If the categories will change twice next quarter, programmatic or prompt-based labels are easier to regenerate than hand labels.&#xD;&#xA;6\. **Can we slice errors by condition?** Systematic errors hide inside averages.&#xD;&#xA;7\. **Who owns review quality?** Someone needs to track honeypot catch rates and acceptance rates, not just throughput.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## If you were starting a dataset on Monday&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Label the first batch by hand, in-house, even if you plan to automate later. The goal isn't saving money. It's finding out what your guidelines got wrong, because the first few hundred items almost always break the taxonomy somewhere. Turn the best of that batch into your golden set before you buy or build any automation.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Then score every label type on the Attention Budget Matrix and decide its workflow on its own terms. Staff the review queue with people who are a little suspicious of the model, give them a one-click reject, and run a honeypot batch whenever the acceptance rate creeps toward 100 percent. Budget for relabeling too. Your label definitions will change, and programmatic labels, an approach \[Snorkel AI](https://snorkel.ai/automated-data-labeling/) and others champion, are far cheaper to regenerate than hand labels.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Who does this work is a separate decision from how it gets done. We cover that side in \[in-house vs outsourced data annotation](/blog/in-house-vs-outsourced-data-annotation/). The bigger shift is already underway: annotators are moving from drawing labels to defining, auditing, and adjudicating them. Fewer people will trace polygons. More will decide what a "successful grasp" means and prove the machine agrees.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## Frequently asked questions&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### Is manual or automated data annotation better for AI training?&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Neither wins across the board. Manual annotation is more reliable on ambiguous, novel, or high-stakes data. Automated annotation is faster and much cheaper at scale on well-defined tasks. Most production teams use a hybrid where models draft labels and humans verify the ones where a mistake would be costly or hard to spot.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### Is manual annotation always more accurate than automated annotation?&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;No. A study of 10 widely used benchmark datasets estimated that about 3.3 percent of human-made test labels were wrong on average, including roughly 6 percent of the ImageNet validation set. Human labels are usually better on hard cases, but they still need consensus checks and audits.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### How much cheaper is automated data annotation?&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;It depends on the task. A PNAS study found ChatGPT text labels cost under $0.003 each, about thirty times less than Amazon Mechanical Turk. Meta's Segment Anything data engine cut mask annotation time to 14 seconds, about 6.5 times faster than COCO-style manual masks. Review and audit time still has to be budgeted.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### What is human-in-the-loop data annotation?&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Human-in-the-loop annotation is a workflow where a model produces draft labels and people review, correct, or reject them. The corrections are fed back to improve the model. It works well when reviewers are trained to challenge the machine instead of approving its output by default.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### When should you avoid automated data labeling?&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Avoid relying on automated labels when a wrong label is costly and also expensive to verify. That includes medical findings, safety-critical edge cases, new object classes the model has never seen, and task-success judgments in robot demonstrations that require watching the full episode with sensor data.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### Can large language models and vision-language models replace human annotators?&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;They can replace a large share of routine labeling, especially text classification and captioning. They don't replace the people who define the label set, build the golden reference data, and audit for systematic errors. Every major automated labeling success so far started from a human-labeled seed.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### How do you check the quality of automated labels?&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Compare them against a human-built golden set, break error rates out by data condition such as lighting or object type, seed known-wrong pre-labels to test whether reviewers catch them, and treat a very high review acceptance rate as a warning sign rather than a success metric.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;### Which annotation approach works best for robotics and physical AI data?&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Robot episodes need a layered approach. Object masks, tracking, and calibration checks automate well. Language instructions can be drafted by a vision-language model and verified by people. Action boundaries, task success, and failure types should stay human-led because they depend on video, force data, and task intent together.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;## Back to the shrink-wrapped pallets&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;Priya's team didn't go back to hand labeling everything. That would have thrown away the large majority of masks the model got right. Instead, they sliced their errors by condition, found the glare cluster, and moved wrapped pallets into the "pre-label, then a human signs off" zone with honeypots in the queue. Everything else stayed automated with sample audits.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;The fix took a week. The lesson took longer to sink in: the model was never the weak point. The review design was.&#xD;&#xA;&#xD;&#xA;So when someone asks whether manual or automated annotation is better, the honest answer is another question.
